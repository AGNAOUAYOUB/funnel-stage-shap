       explainable purchase prediction in e-commerce}

\ead{ay.agnaou@edu.umi.ac.ma}
\author[a]{S.~Ailli}

\begin{abstract}
Purchase prediction from clickstream data is well established, but the
explanations attached to such models are typically computed once, over the
entire session, and are rarely validated. Both practices are
problematic: a whole-session attribution incorporates events at or after the
purchase decision, and it cannot express \emph{when} a behavioural driver
mattered. We propose Funnel-Stage SHAP, a framework in which prediction and
explanation are subject to the same temporal information constraint: at each
funnel stage, an independent model is fitted on the event prefix observable at
that stage's cut-point, Shapley attributions are computed from exactly the
same prefix, and a dedicated validation layer tests whether the resulting
explanations are faithful, stable under perturbation and reproducible across
seeds. Following a design science methodology, the framework is instantiated
on a session-level benchmark (12,330 sessions) and on an event-level
clickstream of 42.4 million events, sessionised into 5.37 million sessions and
subsampled to an analytical cohort of 485,459. Three findings run against
expectation. First, the pre-specified hypothesis that predictive performance
improves along the funnel is not supported under the primary metric: PR-AUC is
non-monotone ($0.131 \to 0.083 \to 0.568$) because chance level rises with it
($\pi_s = 0.073 \to 0.059 \to 0.521$), prevalence-normalised lift falls
monotonically ($1.79 \to 1.09$), and prevalence-free ROC-AUC falls with it
($0.641 \to 0.570$). Normalised precision gain over chance is U-shaped
($0.062 \to 0.026 \to 0.098$), so the direction of the effect is
metric-dependent and we report all three rather than selecting one. Second,
attribution is strongly non-monotone: navigation entropy carries 3.5\% of
attribution mass at exploration, 22.4\% at consideration and 7.4\% at purchase
intent, a trajectory that a whole-session attribution averages into a single
uninformative value. Third, agreement between tree-based and recurrent
attributions holds at the level of aggregate rankings but disappears at the
level of individual journeys. Explanation quality tracks predictive signal, and
we argue that it should therefore be validated per stage rather than per model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================

Every interaction a customer performs on an e-commerce platform---product
searches, page views, category navigation, cart additions---leaves a
behavioural trace, and organisations routinely exploit these traces to
predict whether a browsing session will end in a purchase. The predictions
are acted upon while the session is still unfolding: retargeting budget is
allocated, on-site interventions are triggered, service contacts are
prioritised. Decisions of this kind require reasons, not only scores. A model
that ranks sessions accurately but cannot say \emph{why} offers little
guidance about what to change \citep{rudin2019stop}, and the marketing
literature has long described the online journey as a sequence of
qualitatively distinct phases rather than a single undifferentiated visit
\citep{bucklin2003model, moe2003buying, lemon2016understanding}.

Shapley additive explanations \citep[SHAP;][]{lundberg2017unified}, together
with their exact tree-model variant \citep{lundberg2020local}, have become
the default vehicle for supplying those reasons. In e-commerce they are
typically applied in a particular way: a single model is fitted to
session-level aggregates and a single attribution is computed per feature
over the whole session. This common practice carries two defects, one
methodological and one substantive.

\textbf{The methodological defect is leakage inside the explanation.} When
features are aggregated over an entire session they necessarily include
events at or after the moment of purchase. The canonical benchmark for this
task illustrates the problem directly: its strongest predictor,
\texttt{PageValues}, is the average value of pages on which a transaction was
completed, and is therefore partly a consequence of conversion rather than a
cause of it \citep{sakar2019real}. A model built on such features may predict
well and explain nothing, because the explanation describes the outcome
rather than the behaviour that preceded it. The defect matters operationally
as well as statistically: an explanation that assigns importance to
checkout-page behaviour cannot support an intervention decision taken while
the customer is still browsing product pages, because the attributed
behaviour has not yet occurred.

\textbf{The substantive defect is temporal flattening.} A single attribution
per feature cannot express that a driver mattered at one point in the journey
and not at another. If the importance of a behavioural signal genuinely
changes as intent forms---which the staged view of the consumer journey
predicts---then a whole-session attribution reports its average, a number
that may correspond to no phase of the journey at all.

Both defects share a root cause: prediction and explanation are generated
under different informational regimes. Prefix-based prediction, developed in
predictive process monitoring and early time-series classification, has
established that reliable inference from the observable portion of an
evolving sequence is feasible and that future observations must be excluded
from model inputs \citep{verenich2019survey, wu2021early}. Explanation,
however, has largely escaped the same discipline. Feature attributions
continue to be computed retrospectively from complete sessions, and their
quality is seldom assessed at all: systematic reviews of applied XAI report
that the majority of application papers provide no quantitative evaluation
of the explanations they publish \citep{saarela2024recent,
mainali2023meant}, so the narrower question of whether attributions remain
stable \emph{across} an evolving journey is scarcely posed
\citep{wickramanayake2023generating, mehdiyev2024quantifying}.

\subsection{Positioning and contributions}
\label{sec:positioning}

Sequence-aware Shapley estimation already exists, and this paper does not
propose a new estimator. TimeSHAP extends KernelSHAP to recurrent models and
produces event-, timestep- and feature-level attributions
\citep{bento2021timeshap}; WindowSHAP computes Shapley values over
time-series windows \citep{nayebi2023windowshap}. Our contribution is
framework-level and domain-level, and consists of three parts. The first is
the funnel-stage conditioning of attributions: Shapley values are computed
separately at each funnel stage, on the event prefix observable at that
stage, and the resulting \emph{attribution trajectory} across stages is
characterised, rather than one whole-session attribution being reported.
The second is a validation layer for stage-conditioned explanations, which
tests faithfulness, stability under input perturbation and reproducibility
across random seeds, with thresholds fixed before any result was computed.
The third is cross-paradigm evidence tested two ways: tree-based stage
attributions are compared against recurrent-model attributions both as
aggregate rankings and, critically, at the level of individual journeys.

The study follows a design science research methodology
\citep{hevner2004design} under a written protocol frozen in advance
(v1.0): every analytic decision---research questions, hypotheses, stage
cut-points, feature families, splitting, models, metrics, thresholds and
statistical procedures---was fixed before the test partition was opened, and
each subsequent deviation is recorded with a date, a rationale and the
affected protocol section in a public amendment log (Appendix~A). We
describe this as a pre-specified rather than a publicly registered protocol,
and the distinction is deliberate: the document was version-frozen and is
distributed with the manuscript, but it was not deposited in a
time-stamping registry, so readers should weight it as an auditable record
of intent rather than as third-party-certified priority.
Thirty-five amendments were logged, several of which reverse an initial
decision on the basis of measurement; we regard this record as part of the
contribution rather than an embarrassment to be hidden.

Three empirical findings, each running against the pre-specified
expectation, structure the paper. First, predictive performance does not
improve along the funnel: the primary metric is non-monotone, ranking skill
falls monotonically, and the one normalisation under which late-funnel
performance recovers does so only to a tenth of the available headroom. The
operating regime in which explanations are most needed---early, when
intervention is still possible---is also the regime in which the model
ranks best. Second, attribution mass migrates substantially across
stages, with navigation behaviour peaking mid-funnel; a whole-session
analysis provably flattens this structure. Third, aggregate agreement
between explanation paradigms coexists with near-zero agreement about
individual customers, which cautions against treating aggregate convergent
validity as a licence for instance-level decisions.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews sequential purchase prediction, explainable AI for consumer
analytics, and temporal validity, and states the research gap.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} formalises the conceptual framework.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data, the anti-leakage prefix
protocol, the modelling, tuning and validation procedures.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results against four pre-specified
hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications, limitations
and threats to validity. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Sequential purchase prediction across the customer journey}
\label{sec:related-prediction}

Purchase prediction has evolved from statistical choice models to
architectures that exploit the temporal ordering of customer interactions.
Early work emerged from session-based recommendation, where recurrent
architectures---LSTM and GRU networks---became the dominant approach for
inferring intent from short, often anonymous sessions
\citep{zhang2020learning, zhang2021recurrent, sheu2021knowledge}, with deep
learning surveyed more broadly by \citet{batmaz2018deep}. More recent
advances substitute self-attention for recurrence:
\citet{wang2023transformer} show that combining Transformer and
convolutional components improves session-based recommendation by capturing
global context and local sequential patterns simultaneously.

Tree-based ensemble learning nevertheless remains highly competitive when
behavioural information is represented as structured features. Gradient
boosting methods---XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM
\citep{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost \citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost}---offer
efficient training, robust handling of heterogeneous features and strong
performance on large tabular datasets \citep{ahn2023ensemble}, and empirical
comparisons in event-log prediction confirm that well-configured ensembles
frequently match sequential architectures \citep{tama2020empirical}. Their
limited intrinsic interpretability has, in turn, driven the widespread
adoption of post-hoc explanation, particularly SHAP.

A distinct stream addresses \emph{early} prediction: inferring the outcome
before the sequence completes, so that intervention remains possible.
\citet{wu2021early} formalise the conditions under which early time-series
classification is meaningful, showing that many published approaches rest on
implicit assumptions about the data-generating process that fail in
deployment. Subsequent work jointly optimises accuracy and earliness
\citep{wan2023memory} or incorporates causal structure into streaming
classifiers \citep{cheung2023causal}. Across architectures, however, a
common pattern persists: predictive performance is evaluated on complete
sessions, or explanation is treated as a retrospective exercise performed
after the final prediction. The explanatory side of early prediction---why
the model believes what it believes at each decision point---has received
little attention.

\subsection{Explainable AI for consumer purchase prediction}
\label{sec:related-xai}

SHAP owes its prominence to its game-theoretic foundation: it satisfies
local accuracy, missingness and consistency \citep{lundberg2017unified}, and
TreeSHAP makes exact computation tractable for tree ensembles
\citep{lundberg2020local}. Within consumer analytics, SHAP and related
methods have been applied to customer lifetime value
\citep{bauer2021improved, sun2023research}, relationship-oriented customer
analytics \citep{kitchens2018advanced}, churn, fraud and conversion
prediction. Counterfactual explanation offers a complementary,
action-oriented perspective, identifying minimal feature changes that would
alter a prediction \citep{you2023counterfactual, oliveira2023model,
balbino2023csse, mertes2022ganterfactual}.

Two limitations of this literature motivate the present study. First,
explanation methods are themselves fallible: different explainers may
produce substantially different accounts of the same prediction---the
Rash\^omon effect---and even repeated applications of one explainer can
disagree \citep{mitrut2024clarity}; adversarial constructions can actively
mislead perturbation-based explainers \citep{slack2020fooling}. This argues
for treating explanation quality as an empirical property to be measured,
not assumed \citep{bhatt2020evaluating, hedstrom2023quantus}, and yet
measurement remains the exception rather than the rule in applied work
(Section~\ref{sec:related-limitations}). Second, and more fundamentally,
both attribution and counterfactual methods share a temporal assumption:
explanations are generated from the complete feature representation
available after the session has concluded. Neither paradigm constrains the
explanation to the information actually observable at the decision point it
is meant to support.

\subsection{Temporal validity and prefix-based inference}
\label{sec:related-temporal}

The principle that future observations must not contribute to operational
predictions is firmly established in predictive process monitoring.
\citet{verenich2019survey} benchmark remaining-time prediction across
real-world event logs; \citet{cao2022transition} predict cycle-containing
process durations; \citet{chen2022multi} and \citet{oberdorf2022predictive}
extend prefix-based prediction to multi-task and enterprise-scale settings.
The methodological contribution of this literature is not any particular
architecture but the explicit enforcement of temporal consistency between
the information available at prediction time and the information supplied to
the model.

Extensions of this discipline to \emph{explanation} are scarce.
\citet{wickramanayake2023generating} generate multi-level explanations for
process outcome predictions, and \citet{mehdiyev2024quantifying} quantify
and explain predictive uncertainty in process monitoring, but neither
examines whether attributions computed at prefix length $k$ remain
consistent with those computed at $k+1$. In survival analysis,
time-dependent explanation methods---SurvSHAP(t)
\citep{krzyzinski2022survshap} and SurvLIME \citep{kovalev2020survlime}---model
how feature effects evolve over time, yet they operate on the complete
observed trajectory rather than restricting each explanation to the
information available at successive decision points. Latent-feature saliency
for deep time-series classifiers raises the related concern that
input-space attributions may miss the representations models actually use
\citep{schroder2023latent}. Fairness-aware session-based recommendation
\citep{wu2023faster} likewise adapts model behaviour during the session,
underscoring that operational decisions are made mid-sequence.

\subsection{Limitations of whole-session SHAP for sequential journeys}
\label{sec:related-limitations}

Synthesising these streams, four limitations of conventional SHAP practice
in customer journey analytics can be stated precisely.

\emph{(i) Retrospective computation.} SHAP values are computed after the
model has access to the full feature vector, so explanations describe
completed sessions rather than the information available when an
intervention decision was required.

\emph{(ii) Future-information leakage.} Because the attribution operates on
the complete session, features realised after the decision point receive
non-zero credit. Such attributions are not merely unavailable in
deployment; they are conceptually misleading, since they cite behaviour that
had not yet occurred.

\emph{(iii) Static attribution in a dynamic process.} A single attribution
profile implicitly assumes feature importance is constant across the
journey, an assumption at odds with staged models of consumer behaviour
\citep{bucklin2003model, sismeiro2004modeling, montgomery2004modeling}.

\emph{(iv) Absent validation.} This limitation is the best documented of
the four, and it is documented quantitatively. Reviewing 512
peer-reviewed XAI application articles under a PRISMA protocol,
\citet{saarela2024recent} report that most studies provide no quantitative
assessment of explanation quality, relying instead on anecdotal evidence or
expert opinion, with only roughly one in five employing quantitative
metrics---and that SHAP and LIME are the predominant methods among them.
The pattern is sharper still when attention is restricted to papers that
describe their models as explainable: in a scoping review of 187 such
application papers, \citet{mainali2023meant} find that 81\% conduct no
evaluation whatsoever of the XAI method they adopt, and that 64\% of the
full set apply SHAP or LIME without any evaluation. Among papers that
\emph{introduce} XAI methods---a more methodologically self-conscious
population---\citet{nauta2023anecdotal} still find that one in three
evaluate exclusively with anecdotal evidence, a figure that has improved
only gradually since \citet{adadi2018peeking} observed that 5\% of 381
surveyed papers focused explicitly on evaluation. Against this baseline,
the further question this paper raises---whether attributions remain
faithful, stable and reproducible \emph{across} successive stages of an
evolving journey---is not merely under-studied but largely unposed, since
it presupposes a validation step that most applied work omits entirely.

\subsection{Research gap and positioning}
\label{sec:related-gap}

No existing work jointly delivers temporally valid, stage-specific
explanations \emph{and} evaluates their quality across the journey
(Figure~\ref{fig:gap}). Sequential prediction research supplies temporal
discipline without explanation; consumer-analytics XAI supplies explanation
without temporal discipline; process monitoring supplies prefix-constrained
prediction with, at most, unvalidated prefix explanations. The framework
developed below occupies the intersection: prefix-constrained prediction,
prefix-constrained attribution, and quantitative validation of the
resulting explanations, instantiated end-to-end on a large e-commerce
clickstream.

\section{Conceptual framework}
\label{sec:framework}
%==============================================================================

The framework reconceptualises purchase prediction as a sequence of
temporally constrained decision problems rather than a single classification
task performed after session completion. Its central premise is that
prediction and explanation must satisfy the same information constraint:
each funnel stage is an independent decision problem in which behaviour is
represented by the event prefix observable at that stage, purchase
probability is estimated from that prefix, and the Shapley explanation is
computed from exactly the same representation.
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} presents the architecture. Its essential
property is visible in the topology: both explanation layers---TreeSHAP
over the stage models and TimeSHAP over the recurrent arm---descend from
the \emph{same} nested prefix representation that feeds prediction, rather
than from a separate whole-session view, and no path in the diagram reaches
an explanation without passing through the prefix construction. The
validation layer then consumes both explanation streams, so the framework's
terminal output is not an attribution but a \emph{validated} attribution:
trajectory plus quality evidence. Temporal validity is thus enforced
structurally, at the point where representations are constructed, rather
than procedurally by convention within each analysis. The layers are
formalised below.

\begin{figure*}[t!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{diag1_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Architecture of the Funnel-Stage SHAP framework. Raw clickstream
events are reconstructed into ordered sessions; sessions are partitioned
into nested stage prefixes; an independent model is fitted per stage on its
prefix features; explanations are computed per stage from the same prefix;
and a validation layer tests faithfulness, stability and reproducibility.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Sessions as sequential behavioural processes}
\label{sec:framework-sessions}

Let $\mathcal{S} = \{S_1, \dots, S_N\}$ denote the collection of customer
sessions extracted from the clickstream. Each session is an ordered event
sequence $S_i = (e_1, \dots, e_{T_i})$ in which every event
$e_t = (u_t, t, a_t, p_t, c_t)$ records the customer identifier, timestamp,
interaction type, product and category. The binary outcome
$Y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether the session ends in a purchase. This
representation treats behaviour as a stochastic process observed through
time: the information available for inference expands as events accumulate.

\subsection{Funnel stages and observable event prefixes}
\label{sec:framework-prefixes}

For session $S_i$, let $\tau_k(S_i)$ denote the cut-point associated with
funnel stage $k \in \{1, 2, 3\}$. The observable history at stage $k$ is the
event prefix
\begin{equation}
P_k(S_i) \;=\; \{\, e_t \in S_i : t \le \tau_k(S_i) \,\},
\label{eq:prefix}
\end{equation}
and by construction the prefixes are nested,
$P_1(S_i) \subseteq P_2(S_i) \subseteq P_3(S_i)$: later stages contain all
earlier behavioural evidence together with newly accumulated interactions.
Writing $\mathcal{I}_k = \sigma(P_k)$ for the information set generated by
the stage-$k$ prefix, temporal validity is the requirement that every
stage-$k$ quantity---features, prediction and explanation---be measurable
with respect to $\mathcal{I}_k$. No event occurring after the cut-point may
influence any of them.

\subsection{Stage-specific behavioural representation}
\label{sec:framework-features}

A stage-specific feature mapping
$\Phi_k : P_k \rightarrow \mathbf{x}^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ transforms
each observable prefix into a structured representation covering interaction
counts, temporal pacing, engagement intensity, navigation entropy, browsing
velocity and price context. The defining property is temporal consistency:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}^{(k)} = \Phi_k\!\left(P_k\right),
\qquad
\frac{\partial \mathbf{x}^{(k)}}{\partial e_t} = 0
\;\;\text{for all}\;\; t > \tau_k,
\label{eq:temporal-consistency}
\end{equation}
so that no feature depends on events after the stage cut-point. This
eliminates the principal source of leakage in post-hoc explainability at the
representation level, before any model is fitted.

\subsection{Stage-specific prediction}
\label{sec:framework-prediction}

Rather than a single global decision function, an independent model
$f_k : \mathbb{R}^{d_k} \rightarrow [0, 1]$ is estimated per stage,
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}^{(k)}_i \;=\; f_k\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_i\right)
\;=\; \mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_i = 1 \,\middle|\, \mathbf{x}^{(k)}_i\right),
\label{eq:stage-model}
\end{equation}
with parameters $\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3$ estimated independently.
This acknowledges that the statistical relationship between observed
behaviour and conversion need not be stationary across the journey, and it
makes the stage models conditional by construction: the stage-$k$ model is
fitted on sessions that reached stage $k$, a property whose consequences for
interpretation are developed in Section~\ref{sec:results-descriptive}.

\subsection{Prefix-constrained SHAP explanations}
\label{sec:framework-shap}

For each stage model, TreeSHAP decomposes the prediction additively,
\begin{equation}
f_k(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \phi^{(k)}_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{d_k} \phi^{(k)}_j,
\label{eq:shap}
\end{equation}
where $\phi^{(k)}_0$ is the expected model output over a background sample
and $\phi^{(k)}_j$ the contribution of feature $j$ at stage $k$. Because the
explanation operator $\Psi_k : (\mathbf{x}^{(k)}, f_k) \mapsto
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(k)}$ receives the same prefix-constrained representation
used for prediction, no attribution can cite behaviour that had not yet
occurred when the prediction was produced. Temporal validity extends from
prediction to explanation by construction rather than by convention.

\subsection{Attribution trajectories}
\label{sec:framework-trajectories}

Because independent explanations are produced at successive stages, each
feature (or feature family) $j$ generates a sequence of stage-conditioned
attributions,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_j \;=\; \left(\phi^{(1)}_j,\; \phi^{(2)}_j,\; \phi^{(3)}_j\right),
\label{eq:trajectory}
\end{equation}
its \emph{attribution trajectory}. Trajectories turn explanation from a
static description of one completed prediction into a longitudinal object:
they reveal how predictive evidence emerges, strengthens, weakens or
disappears as the journey unfolds. Whether a trajectory deserves substantive
interpretation, however, is an empirical question---trajectories are only
meaningful if the underlying explanations are faithful, stable and
reproducible, which motivates the validation layer of
Section~\ref{sec:methods-validation}.

\section{Methods and materials}
\label{sec:methods}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Research design}
\label{sec:methods-design}

The study follows a design science research methodology
\citep{hevner2004design}: the objective is not only to investigate a
phenomenon but to construct and rigorously evaluate an artifact---the
Funnel-Stage SHAP framework---that addresses the methodological gap
identified in Section~\ref{sec:related-gap}. Consistent with the DSR
build--evaluate cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:dsr}), the artifact is instantiated
end-to-end and evaluated against pre-specified hypotheses, with every
deviation from the frozen protocol logged with date, rationale and affected
sections (Appendix~A).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{diag4_dsr.pdf}
\caption{The design science research cycle as instantiated in this study:
problem identification, artifact design, instantiation on two datasets,
pre-specified evaluation, and a public amendment log closing the loop.}
\label{fig:dsr}
\end{figure}

The evaluation is organised around four pre-specified hypotheses. H1,
addressing RQ1, expected prevalence-normalised predictive performance to
rise monotonically from exploration to purchase intent as behavioural
evidence accumulates. H2, addressing RQ2, expected engagement-related
features to gain attribution mass across stages while contextual features
lose it. H3 held that navigation entropy and click velocity carry predictive
information beyond baseline aggregate features. H4, addressing RQ3, expected
tree-based and recurrent attributions to agree on their shared concepts,
with Spearman $\rho > 0.6$ as the pre-specified criterion. Every analytic
decision---stage definitions, feature dictionary, splits, seeds, metrics,
thresholds and statistical procedures---was fixed before the test partition
was opened, and the frozen seed list $\{7, 17, 23, 42, 101\}$ (protocol
Appendix~B) governs all seeded procedures.

\subsection{Data sources}
\label{sec:methods-data}

Two complementary datasets serve distinct methodological purposes;
predictive performance obtained on one is not comparable with the other.

\textbf{Benchmark dataset (Dataset A).} The Online Shoppers Purchasing
Intention dataset \citep{sakar2019real}, 12,330 sessions with 15.47\%
prevalence, is used to select the model family under standardised
whole-session conditions and to demonstrate the leakage critique concretely:
its strongest predictor, \texttt{PageValues}, is measured post-transaction.
Because its variables are whole-session aggregates, Dataset~A cannot support
stage-specific prediction; it functions as a benchmark and as the static
comparator, not as an instantiation of the framework.

\textbf{Event-level clickstream (Dataset B).} The primary evaluation uses
the REES46 multi-category e-commerce clickstream (October 2019 partition):
42{,}448{,}764 events with timestamps, interaction types, product, category,
price and user identifiers. Provenance is recorded by SHA-256 checksum, and
all preprocessing is scripted and seeded. Event-level resolution is what
permits the reconstruction of nested behavioural prefixes with temporal
consistency guaranteed at the representation level.

\subsection{Journey reconstruction and stage construction}
\label{sec:methods-journey}

\textbf{Sessionisation.} Events are cleaned, chronologically ordered and
grouped into sessions using a 30-minute inactivity threshold: a new session
begins whenever the gap between consecutive events of the same user exceeds
$\delta = 30$ minutes (sensitivity to $\delta$ is evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity}). The full corpus yields 5{,}366{,}181
sessions; a seeded, user-level subsample of 200{,}000 users---permitted by
the protocol for tractability---yields the analytical dataset of 485{,}459
sessions. Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow} reports the complete data-flow
accounting from raw events to modelled sessions. Two properties of this
accounting matter for the validity of everything downstream. First, every
exclusion is enumerated---malformed events, boundary-straddling users,
sessions failing stage reachability---so the population each model is fitted
on can be reconstructed exactly, and no filtering step can silently shift
the prevalence being modelled. Second, the largest single reduction is
deliberate (the seeded user subsample) rather than incidental, which is what
allows the sample-size sensitivity analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity} to interpret the 400{,}000-user
superset as a controlled enlargement rather than a different draw.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig1_data_flow.pdf}
\caption{Data flow from 42.4M raw events to the analytical dataset. Every
exclusion step is counted; no step discards sessions silently.}

\end{figure}

\textbf{Stage cut-points.} Three modelled stages operationalise the funnel:
S1 (\emph{early exploration}: session entry, cut at the initial catalogue
interactions), S2 (\emph{active consideration}: sustained product/category
browsing), and S3 (\emph{purchase intent}: cart activity begins). The
checkout stage S4 is defined for descriptive statistics but is deliberately
not modelled: its cut-point is the purchase event itself, so any S4 prefix
would contain the label. Cut-point definitions were amended on the basis of
measurement before the test partition was opened; the amendments are logged.
Figure~\ref{fig:prefix} illustrates the construction on a single session:
each stage's model sees only the events to the left of its cut-point, the
three prefix bars nest ($S1 \subseteq S2 \subseteq S3$), and the purchase
event itself sits in the shaded region marked \emph{never in any
prefix}---the portion of the session that conventional whole-session
explanation would consume, excluded here from features, prediction and
attribution alike. The figure also makes the checkout-stage exclusion
self-evident: the purchase event \emph{is} the label, so no admissible
prefix for it exists.

\subsection{Stage-specific feature engineering}
\label{sec:methods-features}

Features are computed independently per stage, exclusively from the stage
prefix, and organised into families: behavioural activity counts (event,
view, cart counts; unique products; revisit rates), temporal dynamics
(prefix duration, inter-event times, dwell times, last gap), behavioural
diversity (category entropy, raw and normalised; distinct-transition rate),
engagement intensity (events per active minute), navigation velocity (click
velocity), contextual price features (mean and maximum price observed), and
calendar context (hour of day, weekend indicator). The resulting matrices
contain 19--23 features per stage with no constant columns; a feature
dictionary with per-stage availability accompanies the repository. Because a
two-event S1 prefix renders several engagement and temporal features
numerically identical, correlated features are grouped for attribution
analysis (Section~\ref{sec:methods-explain}).

\subsection{Splitting and experimental protocol}
\label{sec:methods-splits}

Two protocols are frozen to disk before any model is fitted; every model and
explanation reads the frozen splits, and the test partition is opened
exactly once, at final evaluation.

The \textbf{temporal split} (headline) assigns each user to a period by
their first session and cuts on that boundary, so it is simultaneously
ordered in time and identity-clean---stricter than a purely date-based cut,
which would let a returning visitor appear on both sides. Users whose
activity straddles a boundary are dropped and counted (51{,}256 of 200{,}000
users) rather than reassigned. The \textbf{grouped split} (robustness)
partitions users at random; it is identity-clean but not time-ordered. A
naive random split by session is never used. For Dataset~A, which lacks user
identifiers, sessions are partitioned on month boundaries chosen by
exhaustive search for the split closest to 70/15/15; the December test month
differs distributionally from the training months by a factor of
approximately fifteen in places, a drift whose consequences
Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity} exploits deliberately.

\subsection{Stage-specific prediction, calibration and imbalance}
\label{sec:methods-prediction}

Five model families are evaluated on Dataset~A---logistic regression,
random forest \citep{breiman2001random}, XGBoost, LightGBM and
CatBoost---and LightGBM is carried forward to the stage models, where exact
TreeSHAP is available. Class imbalance is handled by class weighting inside
training folds only; SMOTE \citep{chawla2002smote} was available but is not
used in reported runs.

Probabilities are calibrated with isotonic regression fitted on a dedicated
20\% slice of the \emph{training period}, drawn once per stage so that every
seed and feature set is calibrated on identical rows. Calibrating on the
validation month instead---the textbook location---raises expected
calibration error \citep{guo2017calibration} by an order of magnitude under
the prior shift present in these data
(Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity}); prior-shift correction follows
\citet{saerens2002adjusting}. Operating thresholds are selected on
validation by F1.

\subsection{Hyperparameter optimisation}
\label{sec:methods-tuning}

Headline stage models use the libraries' default hyperparameters. A tuning
study---Optuna \citep{akiba2019optuna} TPE search, 100 trials per stage over
fixed search spaces, maximising validation PR-AUC---is reported as a
robustness analysis (Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity}). A held-out
validation set is used rather than nested cross-validation because the
temporal split forbids shuffling folds across the time boundary. Search
spaces are fixed in source code so a run cannot silently widen them, and
studies persist to reopenable storage. Keeping defaults as the headline
keeps the explanation layers attached to the exact models they explain;
tuning improves S3 and changes no conclusion. Tuning and threshold selection
both touch the validation partition, a double use acknowledged in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Explanation protocol}
\label{sec:methods-explain}

\textbf{Layer 1 --- stage-conditioned TreeSHAP.} Interventional TreeSHAP
\citep{lundberg2020local} is computed per stage against a background sample
drawn from training rows only. Correlated features are clustered by absolute
Spearman correlation and their attributions summed, which is licensed by the
additivity of Shapley values \citep{aas2021explaining}; the clustering is
fixed across stages and taken from the earliest, coarsest stage.
Attributions are computed on the validation partition; the test partition is
reserved for predictive evaluation.

\textbf{Layer 2 --- recurrent attribution.} A GRU is trained per stage on
the \emph{same prefixes} as the tree models and explained with TimeSHAP
\citep{bento2021timeshap}. Training the recurrent arm on whole sessions
would have let it see the purchase event, breaking the prefix protocol for
that arm alone and rendering the cross-paradigm comparison meaningless.

\subsection{Explanation validation layer}
\label{sec:methods-validation}

Layer 3 evaluates explanation quality along four axes
(Figure~\ref{fig:validation}), with pass thresholds fixed in advance:
faithfulness correlation $> 0.5$; cross-seed and cross-paradigm Spearman
$> 0.6$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{diag5_validation.pdf}
\caption{The explanation validation layer: faithfulness (attribution vs.\
measured effect of removal), deletion/insertion curves, stability under
input perturbation (local Lipschitz), and reproducibility across the five
frozen seeds and across explanation paradigms.}
\label{fig:validation}
\end{figure}

\emph{Faithfulness} is the \citet{bhatt2020evaluating} correlation between
attributed importance and the measured change in model output when the
feature is removed, with removal implemented as replacement by a background
value rather than by zero---zero is a valid in-distribution value for most
features and removes no information. \emph{Deletion and insertion curves}
\citep{petsiuk2018rise} order features by signed attribution, since the
claim under test is that removing features that push the prediction up
causes it to fall. \emph{Stability} is a local Lipschitz estimate
\citep{alvarezmelis2018robustness}: the ratio of attribution change to input
perturbation under Gaussian noise. \emph{Consistency} is the Spearman rank
correlation of importance orderings across the five frozen seeds, and
between paradigms (H4)---the latter evaluated both on aggregate rankings and
per instance.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis}
\label{sec:methods-stats}

PR-AUC is the primary metric, as appropriate under class imbalance
\citep{davis2006relationship, saito2015precision}. Because chance-level
PR-AUC equals the prevalence, and prevalence differs by a factor of six
across stages, cross-stage comparison uses \textbf{PR-AUC lift}---PR-AUC
divided by prevalence---alongside the prevalence-free ROC-AUC. Reporting raw
PR-AUC across stages would report prevalence as though it were skill.
Calibration is summarised by expected calibration error
\citep{guo2017calibration}. ROC-AUC differences
on identical test sets use the DeLong procedure
\citep{delong1988comparing, sun2014fast}; PR-AUC differences use stratified
paired bootstrap confidence intervals (2{,}000 resamples). The confirmatory
family of nine stage-level contrasts is Holm-corrected
\citep{holm1979simple}; with five seeds, the minimum attainable Wilcoxon
$p$-value is 0.0625, so seed-level tests are reported descriptively and
inference rests on the bootstrap intervals. All results are reported over
the five frozen seeds as mean $\pm$ standard deviation.

\textbf{H1 is not supported under the pre-specified primary metric, and the
direction of the effect is metric-dependent.} The protocol (\S11) fixes
PR-AUC as primary and ROC-AUC as secondary, and H1 predicted that PR-AUC
would rise monotonically from awareness to intent. It does not: PR-AUC runs
$0.131 \to 0.083 \to 0.568$, falling before it rises, so the monotonicity
H1 asserts fails on the primary metric before any normalisation is
considered. We report the normalised views below because they disagree with
each other, and selecting among them after seeing the results would be the
metric-shopping the pre-specification exists to prevent.
Figure~\ref{fig:curve} makes the performance dynamics visible across stages. In the
left panel, raw PR-AUC ends at 0.568 against a starting 0.131, and a naive
reading would conclude that prediction improves dramatically as customers
approach purchase; but the chance-level curve tracks it almost point for
point, because chance-level PR-AUC equals the prevalence and prevalence
varies six-fold across the conditional stage populations---even the dip
both curves share at S2 is a prevalence phenomenon
(Section~\ref{sec:results-descriptive}), not a modelling one. 

When evaluated via prevalence-normalised metrics, two complementary patterns emerge.
Under \emph{PR-AUC lift} ($\text{PR-AUC}/\pi_s$), performance falls monotonically from 1.79 to 1.09 toward the baseline.
However, because PR-AUC lift has a prevalence-dependent ceiling ($\text{Max Lift} = 1/\pi_s$, imposing a ceiling of $13.7\times$ at S1 vs. $1.92\times$ at S3), lift measures relative multiplicative enhancement over chance rather than absolute distance to perfect precision.
Under \emph{Normalised PR Gain} $(\text{PR-AUC} - \pi_s)/(1 - \pi_s)$ --- which is
algebraically the attainable-lift fraction $\eta$, not an independent
metric --- performance is U-shaped ($0.062 \to 0.026 \to 0.098$), recovering
at Stage 3. The recovery is real but modest in absolute terms: even at its
best the model closes under a tenth of the distance between chance and
perfect precision, and the S3 seed standard deviation ($\pm 0.0086$) is the
largest of the three stages, reflecting that stage's small test partition.
Conversely, prevalence-free \emph{ROC-AUC} drops monotonically from 0.641 to 0.570 with seed standard deviations far smaller than cross-stage differences ($0.0009$--$0.0041$), confirming that the model's pure ranking skill degrades as intent consolidates.
Expected calibration error holds at 0.010--0.017 throughout, so the ranking decline is not a probability calibration artefact.
Two conclusions follow, and it matters that they are kept distinct. The
first is a verdict: H1 predicted improvement and the data show none on the
primary metric, so the hypothesis is rejected, and the ROC-AUC decline
strengthens rather than qualifies that rejection, since it moves in the same
direction on a prevalence-free scale. The second is a caution about
inference: the three normalisations disagree about the shape of the
trajectory, so no single prevalence-adjusted number characterises ``how
predictable'' a stage is. Ranking skill falls monotonically as intent
consolidates; the absolute precision headroom the model captures is smallest
mid-funnel and largest at intent. Both are properties of the same fitted
models, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion-decline} interprets them together
rather than adjudicating between them.

\textbf{H2 is partially supported.} Its behavioural component holds
directionally---engagement gains mass across stages. Its contextual
component is untestable: Dataset~B contains no traffic-source or device
variables, and rather than proxy them we report the sub-hypothesis as
untested. The apparent tension with the ablation---navigation entropy
receives 22.4\% of S2 attribution yet adds no detectable unique predictive
value---is the predicted consequence of correlated families sharing
information (Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}), not a contradiction:
ablation measures unique contribution after accounting for the rest of the
model, while Shapley attribution allocates credit among correlated
features.

Because stage populations differ (Section~\ref{sec:results-descriptive}),
trajectories are read as stage-conditioned explanation profiles rather than
within-session causal evolution; their reliability is established next.

Two results follow, and the first is about prediction rather than

ROC-AUC of 0.9999 at a prevalence of 0.089---effectively perfect
separation, against 0.131 for the best stage model. That gap is not
evidence that whole-session modelling is superior; it is the size of the
leakage. A model permitted to count the events of a completed session can
read the outcome off the session's own length, and its apparent excellence
is an artefact of being asked a question that no deployed system is ever in
a position to ask.

The attribution profile shows where that predictive weight sits. Three
quarters of the whole-session model's explanatory mass falls on quantities
that only exist once the session is over: total event and view counts carry
57.5\% and a purchase-intent composite a further 18.6\%. What remains for
the behavioural families the stage models rely on is correspondingly thin.
Price context, which dominates the exploration stage at 43.7\%, receives
0.2\%. Navigation and category behaviour, which peaks at 22.4\% mid-funnel,
receives 2.5\%. The static analysis does not merely average the trajectory
into a flat value, as an earlier and weaker version of this comparison
assumed; it reallocates attention almost entirely onto session-completion
artefacts and reports the behavioural drivers as near-irrelevant.

\textbf{RQ4 is answered affirmatively, with a qualification about the
comparison itself.} Four features in the whole-session vocabulary
(\texttt{n\_events}, \texttt{n\_views}, \texttt{inter\_event\_std\_s} and
\texttt{purchase\_intent\_score}) fall outside the reference grouping, which
is derived from the earliest and coarsest stage, so they are reported as
their own families rather than merged into the six above. Those four are
precisely the ones carrying the leaked signal, so the qualification does not
weaken the finding; but it does mean the share columns are not a partition
of a common vocabulary, and the flattening column should be read as the loss
of a family's stage-specific prominence rather than as a conserved transfer
of mass.

approximations rather than complete accounts.

\emph{Deletion and insertion} behave as required at every stage: removing
top positively-attributed features degrades the prediction monotonically,
and deletion AUC is roughly half insertion AUC. The attributions point at
features the models genuinely use. These areas are compared within stages
only, since the three models operate on different populations and score
distributions.

\emph{Stability passes at every stage.} The local Lipschitz estimate bounds
attribution change at a maximum ratio of 0.073 (S2), with means near 0.01:
perturbing the input moves the explanation by a small fraction of the
perturbation itself. Notably, S2---weakest on faithfulness---is not
unstable; its explanations are consistent and robust, merely less tethered
to the model's actual sensitivity than at the other stages.

correlations of 0.886 or higher against a threshold of 0.6. This is what

reseeding. The mild decline toward later stages tracks their smaller
evaluation populations.

S2, which combines the weakest predictive lift, the only faithfulness miss,
and (below) the weakest per-instance cross-paradigm agreement.
Consideration is the stage at which this framework's explanations deserve
the most caution, and the paper says so plainly rather than reporting
stage-averaged explanation quality.

\subsection{Cross-paradigm agreement (H4)}
\label{sec:results-h4}

The GRU is competitive with the tree models---test PR-AUC 0.124 against
0.131 at S1 and 0.565 against 0.568 at S3---so disagreement below is not
attributable to one incompetent arm.

At the level of \emph{aggregate rankings}, Spearman correlations between
TimeSHAP and TreeSHAP are $+0.200$ (S1), $+0.500$ (S2) and $+0.600$ (S3)
against a pre-specified threshold of 0.6. \textbf{H4 is not supported},
though the test is severely underpowered: the paradigms share only four or
five mappable concepts, and with four items Spearman takes only a handful of
discrete values. The pre-registration did not anticipate how few concepts
the schemas would share, and the rising trend is therefore read
descriptively.

PR-AUC by $+0.002$ (S1) and $+0.003$ (S2)---within one to two seed standard

All three searches selected the minimum tree count in the space with strong
regularisation---shallow trees at S1 and S3, a slow learning rate at
S2---consistent with a drift-prone temporal split rewarding conservative
models; that the largest gain lands at the underpowered S3 fits the same
reading, and even tuned it remains the weakest stage.

\paragraph{Split protocol} On the grouped split, which is identity-clean
but not time-ordered, lift falls $1.98 \to 1.53 \to 1.15$. The monotone
decline on which H1's rejection rests is therefore not an artefact of the
temporal protocol. Grouped performance sits above temporal at every stage,
which is what temporal drift predicts: a model tested on its own period's
peers faces an easier problem than one tested on the future.

rather than on a slice of the training period raises expected calibration
error from 0.025 to 0.118. Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} shows why the
failure is systematic rather than noisy: the validation-calibrated
reliability curve falls below the identity line in \emph{every} probability
bin---observed conversion rates are uniformly lower than predicted
probabilities---which is the signature of a calibrator transported from a
higher-prevalence population (November, 25.4\%) to a lower-prevalence one
(December, 12.5\%), while the training-slice calibrator (12.2\%) tracks the
identity line closely throughout. The figure is thus a compact empirical demonstration of
prior-probability shift, and the reasons the textbook choice of calibration
set inverts under drift are developed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-method}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Why ranking skill declines as intent forms}
\label{sec:discussion-decline}

The clearest result contradicts the pre-specified hypothesis: predictive
performance does not improve along the funnel, and on the prevalence-free
scale it deteriorates. Because this is a negative result against a
directional expectation, it deserves a substantive rather than merely
statistical interpretation. Two quantities must be held apart first. Ranking
skill---who is more likely to convert than whom---falls monotonically.
Precision headroom captured over chance is U-shaped, recovering at intent.
These are not in conflict: at a base rate of 52\% a model can place many
converters above many non-converters while ordering them poorly, because
the bar chance already clears is high. The interpretation below concerns
ranking skill, which is the quantity an intervention system actually
consumes when it decides whom to target. The pattern admits a selection
reading: each successive stage conditions on an increasingly committed
population, and conditioning on commitment removes precisely the
behavioural variance that discriminates purchasers from non-purchasers.
Early in the journey, broad exploratory signals separate populations that
differ widely in intent; by the time a cart exists, nearly everyone
remaining looks intent, and the residual outcome uncertainty is governed by
factors the clickstream does not observe---price comparison off-site,
household deliberation, payment friction. On this reading, the framework's
falsifiable prediction is that covariates capturing off-stream context
should recover discrimination at S3 where within-stream behaviour cannot;
testing it requires data this study does not possess and is flagged as
future work.

The finding inverts a common operational assumption: the stages where
prediction is conventionally deployed (late, high-intent) are the stages
where the model adds least ranking skill over the base rate, while the
stages where intervention is most valuable (early) are where relative skill
is highest. Prevalence-normalised reporting is what makes this visible;
absolute PR-AUC alone would have told the opposite, wrong, story.
Figure~\ref{fig:contributions} traces this logic for all four research
questions, mapping each from the methodological component that makes it
answerable, through the empirical finding, to the theoretical claim it
licenses. The map makes the design-science character of the study explicit:
each finding is produced by precisely the component---prefix-only features
with lift as the cross-stage metric, stage-conditioned attribution,
pre-specified validation with per-instance agreement, the static
contrast---that conventional pipelines omit, which is the sense in which
the artifact, and not merely the dataset, generates the knowledge. The
map's bottom line is also its epistemic posture: three of the four
pre-specified hypotheses were not supported, and all four are reported as
found.

\subsection{Attribution migration and the staged journey}
\label{sec:discussion-migration}

The migration results give quantitative content to a long-standing
theoretical claim: journey stages are qualitatively distinct decision
regimes \citep{bucklin2003model, lemon2016understanding}. The mid-funnel
peak of navigation entropy is the sharpest illustration---comparison
behaviour is maximally informative exactly when the staged account says
customers compare---and it is structurally invisible to whole-session
attribution, which assigns the family 2.5\% while resting three quarters of
its own explanatory weight on whole-session volume
(Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4}).
The coexistence of high attribution share with negligible unique predictive
contribution (Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}) is itself informative:
attribution answers ``what evidence does the model lean on,'' ablation
answers ``what could not be replaced,'' and conflating the two reads
redundancy as importance or importance as redundancy. Reporting both
quantities side by side, as the ablation ladder and the migration table do
here, makes the distinction inspectable rather than leaving it implicit.

\subsection{Implications for decision support and practice}
\label{sec:discussion-practice}

Three implications follow for intelligent decision support. First,
prediction and explanation should share informational constraints by
architecture, not convention: an explanation layer bolted onto a
whole-session model can cite behaviour that postdates the decision point,
and no amount of visualisation repairs that. Second, intervention design
can key on the stage-conditioned profiles: at exploration, price context
and browsing breadth dominate, favouring assortment and price-transparency
interventions; at consideration, navigation and engagement dominate,
favouring comparison support; at intent, engagement consolidates, and the
model's residual skill is thin---interventions there are better justified
by margin arithmetic than by predicted propensity. Third, explanation
validation belongs in deployment pipelines: the per-stage quality table is
cheap to compute, and in these data it would have flagged S2 as the stage
where explanations deserve least trust---something no aggregate metric
reveals. The per-instance H4 result adds a governance caution: aggregate
agreement between explanation methods does not license per-customer
narratives, and systems that surface individual explanations should
validate at the instance level, not the population level.

\subsection{Methodological implications}
\label{sec:discussion-method}

Two findings generalise beyond this study. The calibration result is a
concrete instance of prior-probability shift \citep{saerens2002adjusting}:
under temporal drift, the validation month is the \emph{worst} place to fit
a calibrator, because its prevalence differs from both training and test;
an ECE of 0.118 versus 0.025 is the measured cost of the textbook choice in
these data. The tuning result carries a related message: under a temporal
split, hyperparameter search consistently selected the most regularised
configurations available, and the gain concentrated in the smallest, most
drift-exposed stage. Both are symptoms of the same underlying regime---
evaluation under distribution shift---and both argue that protocol details
conventionally treated as boilerplate (where the calibrator is fitted,
whether defaults are tuned) interact with drift in ways that are large
relative to the effects under study.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations of this study fall into three groups: what the data cannot
express, what the design conditions on, and what the evaluation cannot yet
establish.

The first group concerns measurement. Search-query text is unavailable in
both datasets, scroll depth is proxied by dwell time, and device and
traffic-source variables are absent from Dataset~B, which is why H2's
contextual arm is reported as untestable rather than approximated through
proxies. Dataset~A, for its part, is non-causal by construction: its
features are whole-session aggregates and \texttt{PageValues} is measured
post-transaction, so it functions in this study strictly as a benchmark and
static comparator, never as an instantiation of the framework.

The second group concerns conditioning and power. The stage models are
conditional on reaching the stage, so the improvement curve is a
conditional claim, and Table~\ref{tab:stages} carries the reach rates and
prevalences without which it cannot be read correctly. The intent stage is
underpowered---with 2{,}973 test sessions no S3 ablation contrast is
decidable---and the temporal split, by keeping visitors whole while
respecting time order, costs 51{,}256 of 200{,}000 users as
boundary-straddlers, retaining 222{,}268 of 485{,}459 sessions. Both
limitations share a single remedy, an observation window longer than one
month, which we regard as the highest-value extension of the present
design. The recurrent arm is likewise small: the GRU is trained briefly on
a subsample, its attributions carry seed standard deviations of 0.2--0.4,
and one single-seed conclusion drawn from it had to be withdrawn
(Appendix~A)---an episode that motivates the sign-stability reporting
standard adopted in Section~\ref{sec:results-h4}.

The third group concerns the boundaries of the evaluation itself. The
validation partition is used twice: hyperparameter tuning selects among
models on validation PR-AUC, and the operating threshold is subsequently
chosen on the same partition. The interaction is weak, since tuning
optimises a threshold-free metric and thresholding is a downstream choice
on the already-selected model, but it is a double use, and a stricter
design would reserve a separate partition for each. More fundamentally, the
framework is predictive, not causal: SHAP quantifies contribution to model
predictions, not causal influence on purchasing, so the trajectories
describe how predictive evidence evolves, not how motives do. Finally,
external validity is bounded by the empirical setting---one retailer, one
market, one month, offline evaluation. The framework is portable; these
specific attribution trajectories are not claimed to be, and live
validation of stage-conditioned interventions remains future work.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

Beyond the extensions above: multi-month windows to relieve S3's power
limitation and reduce boundary attrition; off-stream covariates to test the
falsifiable prediction of Section~\ref{sec:discussion-decline}; finer stage
lattices approaching per-event explanation trajectories; within-method
variability baselines for cross-paradigm comparison; and deployment studies
measuring whether stage-conditioned explanations change intervention
decisions.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

This study asked what happens when explanation is held to the same temporal
standard as prediction. The answer, on 42.4 million events, is that most of
what a whole-session attribution reports is either unavailable at decision
time or an average over regimes the journey keeps distinct. Funnel-Stage
SHAP enforces the prefix constraint at the representation level, conditions
both model and explanation on the funnel stage, and validates the resulting
explanations for faithfulness, stability and reproducibility. Applied
end-to-end, the framework refuted one pre-specified expectation (predictive
performance does not improve along the funnel; ranking skill falls, and the
direction of the effect depends on which normalisation is applied),
quantified a second (attribution mass migrates, with navigation behaviour
peaking mid-funnel), and split a third (explanation paradigms agree in
aggregate and disagree per instance). Each finding is invisible to the
standard pipeline: the first without reporting chance level alongside
performance, the second without stage conditioning, the third without
instance-level validation.

The broader argument is that explanation quality tracks predictive signal
and should be validated where decisions are made---per stage, and where
individual customers are concerned, per instance. As machine learning
systems increasingly operate on evolving behavioural streams, temporal
fidelity is not an optional refinement of explainable AI but a precondition
for explanations that support, rather than merely decorate, operational
decisions.
