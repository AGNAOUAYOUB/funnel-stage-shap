\title{\vspace{-2em}Funnel-Stage SHAP: a design science framework for
       stage-conditioned explainable purchase prediction in e-commerce}
\date{}
\author{}
\maketitle
\vspace{-3em}

\begin{abstract}
\singlespacing
Organisations rank browsing sessions by conversion propensity and act on the
ranking mid-session. A ranking is not a decision: choosing an intervention
requires a reason, a time, and a reference point against which acting is
worthwhile. Conventional practice supplies none, because explanations are
computed once over the completed session and may cite events occurring after
the decision point. We propose Funnel-Stage SHAP, which holds
prediction and explanation to one temporal constraint: at each funnel stage a
model is fitted on the event prefix observable at that stage's cut-point,
attributions are computed from exactly that prefix, and a validation layer
tests their faithfulness, stability and reproducibility. Following a design
science methodology, the artifact is instantiated on 42.4 million clickstream
events yielding 485,459 sessions.

The central finding is that a stage's ranking quality and its targeting value
are distinct quantities that order the stages differently. Across contact
budgets, the worst-ranking stage returns the most conversions per contact once
the budget exceeds roughly 5\% of eligible sessions (incremental precision
$0.114 \pm 0.013$ against $0.093 \pm 0.002$ at a 10\% budget), while
top-decile lift falls monotonically (2.27, 1.64, 1.22). Which stage to target
is unanswerable without naming the budget.

Three pre-specified expectations fail, and two follow-up tests establish what
the failures mean. A fixed-cohort evaluation attributes the declining skill to
population composition rather than to information. A label-permutation null
shows that only the mid-funnel navigation peak (3.5\%, 22.4\%, 7.4\%) carries
attribution a permuted model cannot manufacture.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} explainable AI; SHAP; purchase prediction;
customer journey; clickstream; decision support; design science

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================

The decision this paper supports is a recurring operational one: while a
customer is still browsing, and with only part of the session observed, an
organisation must decide whether to intervene, how, and at what cost. Every
interaction a customer performs on an e-commerce platform---product searches,
page views, category navigation, cart additions---leaves a behavioural trace,
and organisations routinely exploit these traces to rank sessions by
conversion propensity. The rankings are acted upon while the session is still
unfolding: retargeting budget is allocated, on-site interventions are
triggered, service contacts are prioritised.

A ranking, however, is not a decision. Choosing an intervention requires three
things a propensity score does not supply. It requires a \emph{reason}: a
model that ranks accurately but cannot say \emph{why} offers little guidance
about what to change \citep{rudin2019stop}. It requires a \emph{time}: the
marketing literature has long described the online journey as a sequence of
qualitatively distinct phases rather than a single undifferentiated visit
\citep{bucklin2003model, moe2003buying, lemon2016understanding}, and a lever
that works during comparison may be useless at checkout. And it requires a
\emph{reference point}: a targeting rule is worth deploying only insofar as it
beats acting on everyone, which is free. We show in
Section~\ref{sec:decision-support} that this last requirement is not
incidental --- at the cart stage, a model with an apparently strong PR-AUC of
0.568 turns out to refer 99\% of sessions for intervention and to beat a
blanket rule by 0.0008 $F_1$ points. Conventional predictive evaluation ranks
that stage the strongest of the three; a decision-relevant evaluation ranks it
the weakest. The gap between those two verdicts is the space this paper
occupies.

Shapley additive explanations \citep[SHAP;][]{lundberg2017unified}, together
with their exact tree-model variant \citep{lundberg2020local}, have become
the default vehicle for supplying those reasons. In e-commerce they are
typically applied in a particular way: a single model is fitted to
session-level aggregates and a single attribution is computed per feature
over the whole session. This common practice carries two defects, one
methodological and one substantive.

\textbf{The methodological defect is leakage inside the explanation.} When
features are aggregated over an entire session they necessarily include
events at or after the moment of purchase. The canonical benchmark for this
task illustrates the problem directly: its strongest predictor,
\texttt{PageValues}, is the average value of pages on which a transaction was
completed, and is therefore partly a consequence of conversion rather than a
cause of it \citep{sakar2019real}. A model built on such features may predict
well and explain nothing, because the explanation describes the outcome
rather than the behaviour that preceded it. The defect matters operationally
as well as statistically: an explanation that assigns importance to
checkout-page behaviour cannot support an intervention decision taken while
the customer is still browsing product pages, because the attributed
behaviour has not yet occurred.

\textbf{The substantive defect is temporal flattening.} A single attribution
per feature cannot express that a driver mattered at one point in the journey
and not at another. If the importance of a behavioural signal genuinely
changes as intent forms---which the staged view of the consumer journey
predicts---then a whole-session attribution replaces the trajectory with one
number that may correspond to no phase of the journey at all. It is tempting
to assume that number is the trajectory's average. Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4}
shows it is not: a whole-session model fitted on these data reallocates
attribution onto session duration and pace, and reports the stage-specific
drivers as marginal.

Both defects share a root cause: prediction and explanation are generated
under different informational regimes. Prefix-based prediction, developed in
predictive process monitoring and early time-series classification, has
established that reliable inference from the observable portion of an
evolving sequence is feasible and that future observations must be excluded
from model inputs \citep{verenich2019survey, wu2021early}. Explanation,
however, has largely escaped the same discipline. Feature attributions
continue to be computed retrospectively from complete sessions, and their
quality is seldom assessed at all: systematic reviews of applied XAI report
that the majority of application papers provide no quantitative evaluation
of the explanations they publish \citep{saarela2024recent,
mainali2023meant}, so the narrower question of whether attributions remain
stable \emph{across} an evolving journey is scarcely posed
\citep{wickramanayake2023generating, mehdiyev2024quantifying}.

\subsection{Positioning and contributions}
\label{sec:positioning}

Sequence-aware Shapley estimation already exists, and this paper does not
propose a new estimator. TimeSHAP extends KernelSHAP to recurrent models and
produces event-, timestep- and feature-level attributions
\citep{bento2021timeshap}; WindowSHAP computes Shapley values over
time-series windows \citep{nayebi2023windowshap}. Our contribution is
framework-level and domain-level, and consists of three parts. The first is
the funnel-stage conditioning of attributions: Shapley values are computed
separately at each funnel stage, on the event prefix observable at that
stage, and the resulting \emph{attribution trajectory} across stages is
characterised, rather than one whole-session attribution being reported.
The second is a validation layer for stage-conditioned explanations, which
tests faithfulness, stability under input perturbation and reproducibility
across random seeds, with thresholds fixed before any result was computed.
The third is cross-paradigm evidence tested two ways: tree-based stage
attributions are compared against recurrent-model attributions both as
aggregate rankings and, critically, at the level of individual journeys.

The study follows a design science research methodology
\citep{hevner2004design} under a written protocol frozen in advance
(v1.0): every analytic decision---research questions, hypotheses, stage
cut-points, feature families, splitting, models, metrics, thresholds and
statistical procedures---was fixed before the test partition was opened, and
each subsequent deviation is recorded with a date, a rationale and the
affected protocol section in a public amendment log (Appendix~A). We
describe this as a pre-specified rather than a publicly registered protocol,
and the distinction is deliberate: the document was version-frozen and is
distributed with the manuscript, but it was not deposited in a
time-stamping registry, so readers should weight it as an auditable record
of intent rather than as third-party-certified priority.
Forty-three amendments were logged, several of which reverse an initial
decision on the basis of measurement; we regard this record as part of the
contribution rather than an embarrassment to be hidden.

Four empirical findings structure the paper. First, predictive performance
does not improve along the funnel, contrary to the pre-specified
expectation: the primary metric is non-monotone, ranking skill falls
monotonically, and the one normalisation under which late-funnel performance
recovers does so only to a tenth of the available headroom. Second, that
decline is a matter of composition rather than information. When all three
stage models are scored on a single cohort of sessions that reach every
stage, so that prevalence and labels are held identical, the decline
disappears: conditioning on a customer's own prior behaviour, not the
shortness of an early prefix, is what makes the later stages hard. Third,
attribution mass migrates substantially across stages, with navigation
behaviour peaking mid-funnel; a whole-session model fitted on the same data
with the outcome event removed does not reproduce this structure, and
instead concentrates attribution on how long and how fast a session ran.
Fourth, aggregate agreement between explanation paradigms coexists with
near-zero agreement about individual customers, which cautions against
treating aggregate convergent validity as a licence for instance-level
decisions.

A fifth result concerns how these should be used. Ranking quality and
targeting value are separate quantities: swept across contact budgets, the
stage that ranks worst returns the most conversions per contact at budgets
above roughly a twentieth of eligible sessions, while the stage that ranks
best dominates when the budget is small. Which stage to target is therefore
not answerable without naming the budget, and reporting a single
threshold-selected operating point conceals this.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews sequential purchase prediction, explainable AI for consumer
analytics, and temporal validity, and states the research gap.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} formalises the conceptual framework.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data, the anti-leakage prefix
protocol, the modelling, tuning and validation procedures.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results against four pre-specified
hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:decision-support} converts those results into
decision-support terms and carries the paper's principal claim.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications, limitations
and threats to validity. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Sequential purchase prediction across the customer journey}
\label{sec:related-prediction}

Purchase prediction has evolved from statistical choice models to
architectures that exploit the temporal ordering of customer interactions.
Early clickstream work established that browsing behaviour predicts purchase
at the session level \citep{vandenpoel2005predicting}, and the sequential
turn emerged from session-based recommendation, where recurrent
architectures---LSTM and GRU networks---became the dominant approach for
inferring intent from short, often anonymous sessions
\citep{zhang2020learning, zhang2021recurrent, sheu2021knowledge}, with deep
learning surveyed more broadly by \citet{batmaz2018deep}. More recent
advances substitute self-attention for recurrence:
\citet{wang2023transformer} show that combining Transformer and
convolutional components improves session-based recommendation by capturing
global context and local sequential patterns simultaneously.

Tree-based ensemble learning nevertheless remains highly competitive when
behavioural information is represented as structured features. Gradient
boosting methods---XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM
\citep{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost \citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost}---offer
efficient training, robust handling of heterogeneous features and strong
performance on large tabular datasets \citep{ahn2023ensemble}, and empirical
comparisons in event-log prediction confirm that well-configured ensembles
frequently match sequential architectures \citep{tama2020empirical}. Their
limited intrinsic interpretability has, in turn, driven the widespread
adoption of post-hoc explanation, particularly SHAP.

A distinct stream addresses \emph{early} prediction: inferring the outcome
before the sequence completes, so that intervention remains possible.
\citet{wu2021early} formalise the conditions under which early time-series
classification is meaningful, showing that many published approaches rest on
implicit assumptions about the data-generating process that fail in
deployment. Subsequent work jointly optimises accuracy and earliness
\citep{wan2023memory} or incorporates causal structure into streaming
classifiers \citep{cheung2023causal}. Across architectures, however, a
common pattern persists: predictive performance is evaluated on complete
sessions, or explanation is treated as a retrospective exercise performed
after the final prediction. The explanatory side of early prediction---why
the model believes what it believes at each decision point---has received
little attention.

\subsection{Explainable AI for consumer purchase prediction}
\label{sec:related-xai}

SHAP owes its prominence to its game-theoretic foundation: it satisfies
local accuracy, missingness and consistency \citep{lundberg2017unified}, and
TreeSHAP makes exact computation tractable for tree ensembles
\citep{lundberg2020local}. Within consumer analytics, SHAP and related
methods have been applied to customer lifetime value
\citep{bauer2021improved, sun2023research}, relationship-oriented customer
analytics \citep{kitchens2018advanced}, churn, fraud and conversion
prediction. Counterfactual explanation offers a complementary,
action-oriented perspective, identifying minimal feature changes that would
alter a prediction \citep{you2023counterfactual, oliveira2023model,
balbino2023csse, mertes2022ganterfactual}.

Two limitations of this literature motivate the present study. First,
explanation methods are themselves fallible: different explainers may
produce substantially different accounts of the same prediction---the
Rash\^omon effect---and even repeated applications of one explainer can
disagree \citep{mitrut2024clarity}; adversarial constructions can actively
mislead perturbation-based explainers \citep{slack2020fooling}. This argues
for treating explanation quality as an empirical property to be measured,
not assumed \citep{bhatt2020evaluating, hedstrom2023quantus}, and yet
measurement remains the exception rather than the rule in applied work
(Section~\ref{sec:related-limitations}). Second, and more fundamentally,
both attribution and counterfactual methods share a temporal assumption:
explanations are generated from the complete feature representation
available after the session has concluded. Neither paradigm constrains the
explanation to the information actually observable at the decision point it
is meant to support.

\subsection{Temporal validity and prefix-based inference}
\label{sec:related-temporal}

The principle that future observations must not contribute to operational
predictions is firmly established in predictive process monitoring.
\citet{verenich2019survey} benchmark remaining-time prediction across
real-world event logs; \citet{cao2022transition} predict cycle-containing
process durations; \citet{chen2022multi} and \citet{oberdorf2022predictive}
extend prefix-based prediction to multi-task and enterprise-scale settings.
The methodological contribution of this literature is not any particular
architecture but the explicit enforcement of temporal consistency between
the information available at prediction time and the information supplied to
the model.

Extensions of this discipline to \emph{explanation} are scarce.
\citet{wickramanayake2023generating} generate multi-level explanations for
process outcome predictions, and \citet{mehdiyev2024quantifying} quantify
and explain predictive uncertainty in process monitoring, but neither
examines whether attributions computed at prefix length $k$ remain
consistent with those computed at $k+1$. In survival analysis,
time-dependent explanation methods---SurvSHAP(t)
\citep{krzyzinski2022survshap} and SurvLIME \citep{kovalev2020survlime}---model
how feature effects evolve over time, yet they operate on the complete
observed trajectory rather than restricting each explanation to the
information available at successive decision points. Latent-feature saliency
for deep time-series classifiers raises the related concern that
input-space attributions may miss the representations models actually use
\citep{schroder2023latent}. Fairness-aware session-based recommendation
\citep{wu2023faster} likewise adapts model behaviour during the session,
underscoring that operational decisions are made mid-sequence.

\subsection{Limitations of whole-session SHAP for sequential journeys}
\label{sec:related-limitations}

Synthesising these streams, four limitations of conventional SHAP practice
in customer journey analytics can be stated precisely.

\emph{(i) Retrospective computation.} SHAP values are computed after the
model has access to the full feature vector, so explanations describe
completed sessions rather than the information available when an
intervention decision was required.

\emph{(ii) Future-information leakage.} Because the attribution operates on
the complete session, features realised after the decision point receive
non-zero credit. Such attributions are not merely unavailable in
deployment; they are conceptually misleading, since they cite behaviour that
had not yet occurred.

\emph{(iii) Static attribution in a dynamic process.} A single attribution
profile implicitly assumes feature importance is constant across the
journey, an assumption at odds with staged models of consumer behaviour
\citep{bucklin2003model, sismeiro2004modeling, montgomery2004modeling}.

\emph{(iv) Absent validation.} This limitation is the best documented of
the four, and it is documented quantitatively. Reviewing 512
peer-reviewed XAI application articles under a PRISMA protocol,
\citet{saarela2024recent} report that most studies provide no quantitative
assessment of explanation quality, relying instead on anecdotal evidence or
expert opinion, with only roughly one in five employing quantitative
metrics---and that SHAP and LIME are the predominant methods among them.
The pattern is sharper still when attention is restricted to papers that
describe their models as explainable: in a scoping review of 187 such
application papers, \citet{mainali2023meant} find that 81\% conduct no
evaluation whatsoever of the XAI method they adopt, and that 64\% of the
full set apply SHAP or LIME without any evaluation. Among papers that
\emph{introduce} XAI methods---a more methodologically self-conscious
population---\citet{nauta2023anecdotal} still find that one in three
evaluate exclusively with anecdotal evidence, a figure that has improved
only gradually since \citet{adadi2018peeking} observed that 5\% of 381
surveyed papers focused explicitly on evaluation. Against this baseline,
the further question this paper raises---whether attributions remain
faithful, stable and reproducible \emph{across} successive stages of an
evolving journey---is not merely under-studied but largely unposed, since
it presupposes a validation step that most applied work omits entirely.

\subsection{Targeting value versus predictive accuracy}
\label{sec:related-uplift}

A separate literature has long argued that ranking customers by predicted
outcome is the wrong basis for deciding whom to contact. \citet{ascarza2018retention}
shows, in two field experiments, that targeting the customers most likely to
churn can be ineffective or counterproductive, because propensity to
experience an outcome is not the same as responsiveness to an intervention.
Uplift modelling formalises the distinction by estimating the treatment
effect directly rather than the outcome probability
\citep{devriendt2018literature}, and a parallel strand argues that the
evaluation metric must also change: \citet{verbraken2013novel} propose a
profit-maximising criterion for churn classifiers,
\citet{lemmens2020managing} show that optimising for profit rather than
accuracy changes which customers a retention programme should target, and
\citet{gubela2021uplift} develop value-driven metrics for uplift models in
this journal.

This paper is not an uplift study, and the distinction should be stated
plainly because it bounds what the results mean. Estimating uplift requires
randomised exposure to an intervention, and the observational clickstream
used here contains none: no session was assigned to a treatment, so no
treatment effect is identified. What the analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:decision-support} supplies is the quantity that sits one
step before uplift --- how much better than a blanket rule a stage-specific
model can select sessions, measured in conversions per contact at a stated
budget. That quantity is necessary but not sufficient for a targeting
decision: a stage at which the model cannot beat contacting everyone cannot
be worth targeting, whatever the uplift, whereas a stage at which it can may
still fail on uplift grounds if the sessions it selects would have converted
anyway. Two connections to the present work follow. First, the
prevalence-normalised reporting argued for here is the same move the
profit-driven literature makes for a different reason, and the two are
complementary: normalising against chance identifies where a model has
selective power, and uplift modelling then identifies whether exercising it
changes behaviour. Second, the temporal constraint this paper imposes applies
to uplift models as directly as to propensity models, since an uplift
estimate computed from post-decision features is unusable for the same reason
a retrospective attribution is.

\subsection{Research gap and positioning}
\label{sec:related-gap}

No existing work jointly delivers temporally valid, stage-specific
explanations \emph{and} evaluates their quality across the journey
(Figure~\ref{fig:gap}). Sequential prediction research supplies temporal
discipline without explanation; consumer-analytics XAI supplies explanation
without temporal discipline; process monitoring supplies prefix-constrained
prediction with, at most, unvalidated prefix explanations. The framework
developed below occupies the intersection: prefix-constrained prediction,
prefix-constrained attribution, and quantitative validation of the
resulting explanations, instantiated end-to-end on a large e-commerce
clickstream.

\section{Conceptual framework}
\label{sec:framework}
%==============================================================================

The framework reconceptualises purchase prediction as a sequence of
temporally constrained decision problems rather than a single classification
task performed after session completion. Its central premise is that
prediction and explanation must satisfy the same information constraint:
each funnel stage is an independent decision problem in which behaviour is
represented by the event prefix observable at that stage, purchase
probability is estimated from that prefix, and the Shapley explanation is
computed from exactly the same representation.
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} presents the architecture. Its essential
property is visible in the topology: both explanation layers---TreeSHAP
over the stage models and TimeSHAP over the recurrent arm---descend from
the \emph{same} nested prefix representation that feeds prediction, rather
than from a separate whole-session view, and no path in the diagram reaches
an explanation without passing through the prefix construction. The
validation layer then consumes both explanation streams, so the framework's
terminal output is not an attribution but a \emph{validated} attribution:
trajectory plus quality evidence. Temporal validity is thus enforced
structurally, at the point where representations are constructed, rather
than procedurally by convention within each analysis. The layers are
formalised below.

% [fig:architecture moved to supplementary material]

\subsection{Sessions as sequential behavioural processes}
\label{sec:framework-sessions}

Let $\mathcal{S} = \{S_1, \dots, S_N\}$ denote the collection of customer
sessions extracted from the clickstream. Each session is an ordered event
sequence $S_i = (e_1, \dots, e_{T_i})$ in which every event
$e_t = (u_t, t, a_t, p_t, c_t)$ records the customer identifier, timestamp,
interaction type, product and category. The binary outcome
$Y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether the session ends in a purchase. This
representation treats behaviour as a stochastic process observed through
time: the information available for inference expands as events accumulate.

\subsection{Funnel stages and observable event prefixes}
\label{sec:framework-prefixes}

For session $S_i$, let $\tau_k(S_i)$ denote the cut-point associated with
funnel stage $k \in \{1, 2, 3\}$. The observable history at stage $k$ is the
event prefix
\begin{equation}
P_k(S_i) \;=\; \{\, e_t \in S_i : t \le \tau_k(S_i) \,\},
\label{eq:prefix}
\end{equation}
and by construction the prefixes are nested,
$P_1(S_i) \subseteq P_2(S_i) \subseteq P_3(S_i)$: later stages contain all
earlier behavioural evidence together with newly accumulated interactions.
Writing $\mathcal{I}_k = \sigma(P_k)$ for the information set generated by
the stage-$k$ prefix, temporal validity is the requirement that every
stage-$k$ quantity---features, prediction and explanation---be measurable
with respect to $\mathcal{I}_k$. No event occurring after the cut-point may
influence any of them.

\subsection{Stage-specific behavioural representation}
\label{sec:framework-features}

A stage-specific feature mapping
$\Phi_k : P_k \rightarrow \mathbf{x}^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ transforms
each observable prefix into a structured representation covering interaction
counts, temporal pacing, engagement intensity, navigation entropy, browsing
velocity and price context. The defining property is temporal consistency:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}^{(k)} = \Phi_k\!\left(P_k\right),
\qquad
\frac{\partial \mathbf{x}^{(k)}}{\partial e_t} = 0
\;\;\text{for all}\;\; t > \tau_k,
\label{eq:temporal-consistency}
\end{equation}
so that no feature depends on events after the stage cut-point. This
eliminates the principal source of leakage in post-hoc explainability at the
representation level, before any model is fitted.

\subsection{Stage-specific prediction}
\label{sec:framework-prediction}

Rather than a single global decision function, an independent model
$f_k : \mathbb{R}^{d_k} \rightarrow [0, 1]$ is estimated per stage,
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}^{(k)}_i \;=\; f_k\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_i\right)
\;=\; \mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_i = 1 \,\middle|\, \mathbf{x}^{(k)}_i\right),
\label{eq:stage-model}
\end{equation}
with parameters $\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3$ estimated independently.
This acknowledges that the statistical relationship between observed
behaviour and conversion need not be stationary across the journey, and it
makes the stage models conditional by construction: the stage-$k$ model is
fitted on sessions that reached stage $k$, a property whose consequences for
interpretation are developed in Section~\ref{sec:results-descriptive}.

\subsection{Prefix-constrained SHAP explanations}
\label{sec:framework-shap}

For each stage model, TreeSHAP decomposes the prediction additively,
\begin{equation}
f_k(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \phi^{(k)}_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{d_k} \phi^{(k)}_j,
\label{eq:shap}
\end{equation}
where $\phi^{(k)}_0$ is the expected model output over a background sample
and $\phi^{(k)}_j$ the contribution of feature $j$ at stage $k$. Because the
explanation operator $\Psi_k : (\mathbf{x}^{(k)}, f_k) \mapsto
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(k)}$ receives the same prefix-constrained representation
used for prediction, no attribution can cite behaviour that had not yet
occurred when the prediction was produced. Temporal validity extends from
prediction to explanation by construction rather than by convention.

\subsection{Attribution trajectories}
\label{sec:framework-trajectories}

Because independent explanations are produced at successive stages, each
feature (or feature family) $j$ generates a sequence of stage-conditioned
attributions,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_j \;=\; \left(\phi^{(1)}_j,\; \phi^{(2)}_j,\; \phi^{(3)}_j\right),
\label{eq:trajectory}
\end{equation}
its \emph{attribution trajectory}. Trajectories turn explanation from a
static description of one completed prediction into a longitudinal object:
they reveal how predictive evidence emerges, strengthens, weakens or
disappears as the journey unfolds. Whether a trajectory deserves substantive
interpretation, however, is an empirical question---trajectories are only
meaningful if the underlying explanations are faithful, stable and
reproducible, which motivates the validation layer of
Section~\ref{sec:methods-validation}.

\section{Methods and materials}
\label{sec:methods}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Research design}
\label{sec:methods-design}

The study follows a design science research methodology
\citep{hevner2004design}: the objective is not only to investigate a
phenomenon but to construct and rigorously evaluate an artifact---the
Funnel-Stage SHAP framework---that addresses the methodological gap
identified in Section~\ref{sec:related-gap}. Consistent with the DSR
build--evaluate--refine cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:dsr}), the artifact is
instantiated end-to-end and evaluated against pre-specified hypotheses, with
every deviation from the frozen protocol logged with date, rationale and
affected sections (Appendix~A).

\textbf{The study runs two cycles, and the distinction organises what follows.}
Cycle one is confirmatory: the artifact as designed, evaluated against the four
hypotheses fixed before the test partition was opened
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-performance}--\ref{sec:results-h4}). Cycle two is
the refinement that cycle one's results demanded. Three of its evaluations
were not anticipated by the protocol because the questions they answer were
not visible until the first cycle produced its results: a fixed-cohort
evaluation, once the funnel decline proved to confound information with
population composition
(Section~\ref{sec:results-cohort}); a whole-session comparator with the
outcome event removed, once the first such comparator proved to contain the
label (Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4}); and a sweep over contact budgets, once
the pre-specified threshold rule proved to determine the decision conclusion
(Section~\ref{sec:decision-economics}). These are labelled exploratory
throughout and listed in Appendix~\ref{app:alignment}. Refinement prompted by
evaluation is what the DSR cycle prescribes, so the label marks where in the
cycle a result sits rather than qualifying its standing --- but it does mean
that cycle-two results are hypothesis-generating, and a reader weighting
confirmatory and exploratory evidence differently should weight them
accordingly.

% [fig:dsr moved to supplementary material]

The evaluation is organised around four pre-specified hypotheses. H1,
addressing RQ1, expected prevalence-normalised predictive performance to
rise monotonically from exploration to purchase intent as behavioural
evidence accumulates. H2, addressing RQ2, expected engagement-related
features to gain attribution mass across stages while contextual features
lose it. H3 held that navigation entropy and click velocity carry predictive
information beyond baseline aggregate features. H4, addressing RQ3, expected
tree-based and recurrent attributions to agree on their shared concepts,
with Spearman $\rho > 0.6$ as the pre-specified criterion. Every analytic
decision---stage definitions, feature dictionary, splits, seeds, metrics,
thresholds and statistical procedures---was fixed before the test partition
was opened, and the frozen seed list $\{7, 17, 23, 42, 101\}$ (protocol
Appendix~B) governs all seeded procedures.

\subsection{Data sources}
\label{sec:methods-data}

Two complementary datasets serve distinct methodological purposes;
predictive performance obtained on one is not comparable with the other.

\textbf{Benchmark dataset (Dataset A).} The Online Shoppers Purchasing
Intention dataset \citep{sakar2019real}, 12,330 sessions with 15.47\%
prevalence, is used to select the model family under standardised
whole-session conditions and to demonstrate the leakage critique concretely:
its strongest predictor, \texttt{PageValues}, is measured post-transaction.
Because its variables are whole-session aggregates, Dataset~A cannot support
stage-specific prediction; it functions as a benchmark and as the static
comparator, not as an instantiation of the framework.

\textbf{Event-level clickstream (Dataset B).} The primary evaluation uses
the REES46 multi-category e-commerce clickstream (October 2019 partition):
42{,}448{,}764 events with timestamps, interaction types, product, category,
price and user identifiers. Provenance is recorded by SHA-256 checksum, and
all preprocessing is scripted and seeded. Event-level resolution is what
permits the reconstruction of nested behavioural prefixes with temporal
consistency guaranteed at the representation level.

\subsection{Journey reconstruction and stage construction}
\label{sec:methods-journey}

\textbf{Sessionisation.} Events are cleaned, chronologically ordered and
grouped into sessions using a 30-minute inactivity threshold: a new session
begins whenever the gap between consecutive events of the same user exceeds
$\delta = 30$ minutes (sensitivity to $\delta$ is evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity}). The full corpus yields 5{,}366{,}181
sessions; a seeded, user-level subsample of 200{,}000 users---permitted by
the protocol for tractability---yields the analytical dataset of 485{,}459
sessions. Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow} reports the complete data-flow
accounting from raw events to modelled sessions. Two properties of this
accounting matter for the validity of everything downstream. First, every
exclusion is enumerated---malformed events, boundary-straddling users,
sessions failing stage reachability---so the population each model is fitted
on can be reconstructed exactly, and no filtering step can silently shift
the prevalence being modelled. Second, the largest single reduction is
deliberate (the seeded user subsample) rather than incidental, which is what
allows the sample-size sensitivity analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity} to interpret the 400{,}000-user
superset as a controlled enlargement rather than a different draw.

% [fig:dataflow moved to supplementary material]

\textbf{Stage cut-points.} Three modelled stages operationalise the funnel:
S1 (\emph{early exploration}: session entry, cut at the initial catalogue
interactions), S2 (\emph{active consideration}: sustained product/category
browsing), and S3 (\emph{purchase intent}: cart activity begins). The
checkout stage S4 is defined for descriptive statistics but is deliberately
not modelled: its cut-point is the purchase event itself, so any S4 prefix
would contain the label. Cut-point definitions were amended on the basis of
measurement before the test partition was opened; the amendments are logged.
Figure~\ref{fig:prefix} illustrates the construction on a single session:
each stage's model sees only the events to the left of its cut-point, the
three prefix bars nest ($S1 \subseteq S2 \subseteq S3$), and the purchase
event itself sits in the shaded region marked \emph{never in any
prefix}---the portion of the session that conventional whole-session
explanation would consume, excluded here from features, prediction and
attribution alike. The figure also makes the checkout-stage exclusion
self-evident: the purchase event \emph{is} the label, so no admissible
prefix for it exists.

\subsection{Stage-specific feature engineering}
\label{sec:methods-features}

Features are computed independently per stage, exclusively from the stage
prefix, and organised into families: behavioural activity counts (event,
view, cart counts; unique products; revisit rates), temporal dynamics
(prefix duration, inter-event times, dwell times, last gap), behavioural
diversity (category entropy, raw and normalised; distinct-transition rate),
engagement intensity (events per active minute), navigation velocity (click
velocity), contextual price features (mean and maximum price observed), and
calendar context (hour of day, weekend indicator). The resulting matrices
contain 19--23 features per stage with no constant columns; a feature
dictionary with per-stage availability accompanies the repository. Because a
two-event S1 prefix renders several engagement and temporal features
numerically identical, correlated features are grouped for attribution
analysis (Section~\ref{sec:methods-explain}).

\subsection{Splitting and experimental protocol}
\label{sec:methods-splits}

Two protocols are frozen to disk before any model is fitted; every model and
explanation reads the frozen splits, and the test partition is opened
exactly once, at final evaluation.

The \textbf{temporal split} (headline) assigns each user to a period by
their first session and cuts on that boundary, so it is simultaneously
ordered in time and identity-clean---stricter than a purely date-based cut,
which would let a returning visitor appear on both sides. Users whose
activity straddles a boundary are dropped and counted (51{,}256 of 200{,}000
users) rather than reassigned. The \textbf{grouped split} (robustness)
partitions users at random; it is identity-clean but not time-ordered. A
naive random split by session is never used. For Dataset~A, which lacks user
identifiers, sessions are partitioned on month boundaries chosen by
exhaustive search for the split closest to 70/15/15; the December test month
differs distributionally from the training months by a factor of
approximately fifteen in places, a drift whose consequences
Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity} exploits deliberately.

\subsection{Stage-specific prediction, calibration and imbalance}
\label{sec:methods-prediction}

Five model families are evaluated on Dataset~A---logistic regression,
random forest \citep{breiman2001random}, XGBoost, LightGBM and
CatBoost---and LightGBM is carried forward to the stage models, where exact
TreeSHAP is available. Class imbalance is handled by class weighting inside
training folds only; SMOTE \citep{chawla2002smote} was available but is not
used in reported runs.

Probabilities are calibrated with isotonic regression
\citep{niculescu2005predicting} fitted on a dedicated
20\% slice of the \emph{training period}, drawn once per stage so that every
seed and feature set is calibrated on identical rows. Calibrating on the
validation month instead---the textbook location---raises expected
calibration error \citep{guo2017calibration} by an order of magnitude under
the prior shift present in these data
(Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity}); prior-shift correction follows
\citet{saerens2002adjusting}. Operating thresholds are selected on
validation by F1.

\subsection{Hyperparameter optimisation}
\label{sec:methods-tuning}

Headline stage models use the libraries' default hyperparameters. A tuning
study---Optuna \citep{akiba2019optuna} TPE search, 100 trials per stage over
fixed search spaces, maximising validation PR-AUC---is reported as a
robustness analysis (Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity}). A held-out
validation set is used rather than nested cross-validation because the
temporal split forbids shuffling folds across the time boundary. Search
spaces are fixed in source code so a run cannot silently widen them, and
studies persist to reopenable storage. Keeping defaults as the headline
keeps the explanation layers attached to the exact models they explain;
tuning improves S3 and changes no conclusion. Tuning and threshold selection
both touch the validation partition, a double use acknowledged in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Explanation protocol}
\label{sec:methods-explain}

\textbf{Layer 1 --- stage-conditioned TreeSHAP.} Interventional TreeSHAP
\citep{lundberg2020local} is computed per stage against a background sample
drawn from training rows only. Correlated features are clustered by absolute
Spearman correlation and their attributions summed, which is licensed by the
additivity of Shapley values \citep{aas2021explaining}; the clustering is
fixed across stages and taken from the earliest, coarsest stage.
Attributions are computed on the validation partition; the test partition is
reserved for predictive evaluation.

\textbf{Layer 2 --- recurrent attribution.} A GRU is trained per stage on
the \emph{same prefixes} as the tree models and explained with TimeSHAP
\citep{bento2021timeshap}. Training the recurrent arm on whole sessions
would have let it see the purchase event, breaking the prefix protocol for
that arm alone and rendering the cross-paradigm comparison meaningless.

The recurrent arm is specified in full because the cross-paradigm comparison
is only interpretable if the recurrent model is competently trained; a weak
GRU would produce disagreement that says nothing about representation. The
architecture is two stacked GRU layers with hidden size 128 and dropout 0.2
between layers and before a single linear output head, consuming ordered,
padded event sequences. Training uses class-weighted binary cross-entropy,
Adam at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ with cosine-annealing decay to
$10^{-5}$, batch size 512, and gradient clipping at unit norm. Training runs
for up to 80 epochs with early stopping on \emph{validation} PR-AUC at a
patience of 10, restoring the best checkpoint; sequences are drawn from up to
60{,}000 sessions per stage. An earlier revision of this study trained for a
fixed eight epochs without early stopping, and the convergence criterion
above replaced it (amendment A30) precisely because a comparison against an
undertrained recurrent model would have been uninformative. The resulting
models are competitive with the trees on the primary metric --- test PR-AUC
0.124 against 0.131 at S1 and 0.565 against 0.568 at S3 --- which is the
evidence that the disagreement reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-h4} is
not an artefact of one weak arm.

\subsection{Explanation validation layer}
\label{sec:methods-validation}

Layer 3 evaluates explanation quality along four axes
(Figure~\ref{fig:validation}), with pass thresholds fixed in advance:
faithfulness correlation $> 0.5$; cross-seed and cross-paradigm Spearman
$> 0.6$.

% [fig:validation moved to supplementary material]

\emph{Faithfulness} is the \citet{bhatt2020evaluating} correlation between
attributed importance and the measured change in model output when the
feature is removed, with removal implemented as replacement by a background
value rather than by zero---zero is a valid in-distribution value for most
features and removes no information. \emph{Deletion and insertion curves}
\citep{petsiuk2018rise} order features by signed attribution, since the
claim under test is that removing features that push the prediction up
causes it to fall. \emph{Stability} is a local Lipschitz estimate
\citep{alvarezmelis2018robustness}: the ratio of attribution change to input
perturbation under Gaussian noise. \emph{Consistency} is the Spearman rank
correlation of importance orderings across the five frozen seeds, and
between paradigms (H4)---the latter evaluated both on aggregate rankings and
per instance.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis}
\label{sec:methods-stats}

PR-AUC is the primary metric, as appropriate under class imbalance
\citep{davis2006relationship, saito2015precision}. Because chance-level
PR-AUC equals the prevalence, and prevalence differs by a factor of six
across stages, cross-stage comparison uses \textbf{PR-AUC lift}---PR-AUC
divided by prevalence---alongside the prevalence-free ROC-AUC. Reporting raw
PR-AUC across stages would report prevalence as though it were skill.
Calibration is summarised by expected calibration error
\citep{guo2017calibration}. ROC-AUC differences
on identical test sets use the DeLong procedure
\citep{delong1988comparing, sun2014fast}; PR-AUC differences use stratified
paired bootstrap confidence intervals (2{,}000 resamples). The confirmatory
family of nine stage-level contrasts is Holm-corrected
\citep{holm1979simple}, a familywise procedure chosen over false-discovery
control \citep{benjamini1995controlling} because the family is small and the
conclusions are directional; with five seeds, the minimum attainable Wilcoxon
$p$-value is 0.0625, so seed-level tests are reported descriptively and
inference rests on the bootstrap intervals. All results are reported over
the five frozen seeds as mean $\pm$ standard deviation.

\textbf{H1 is not supported under the pre-specified primary metric, and the
direction of the effect is metric-dependent.} The protocol (\S11) fixes
PR-AUC as primary and ROC-AUC as secondary, and H1 predicted that PR-AUC
would rise monotonically from awareness to intent. It does not: PR-AUC runs
$0.131 \to 0.083 \to 0.568$, falling before it rises, so the monotonicity
H1 asserts fails on the primary metric before any normalisation is
considered. We report the normalised views below because they disagree with
each other, and selecting among them after seeing the results would be the
metric-shopping the pre-specification exists to prevent.
Figure~\ref{fig:curve} makes the performance dynamics visible across stages. In the
left panel, raw PR-AUC ends at 0.568 against a starting 0.131, and a naive
reading would conclude that prediction improves dramatically as customers
approach purchase; but the chance-level curve tracks it almost point for
point, because chance-level PR-AUC equals the prevalence and prevalence
varies six-fold across the conditional stage populations---even the dip
both curves share at S2 is a prevalence phenomenon
(Section~\ref{sec:results-descriptive}), not a modelling one. 

When evaluated via prevalence-normalised metrics, two complementary patterns emerge.
Under \emph{PR-AUC lift} ($\text{PR-AUC}/\pi_s$), performance falls monotonically from 1.79 to 1.09 toward the baseline.
However, because PR-AUC lift has a prevalence-dependent ceiling ($\text{Max Lift} = 1/\pi_s$, imposing a ceiling of $13.7\times$ at S1 vs. $1.92\times$ at S3), lift measures relative multiplicative enhancement over chance rather than absolute distance to perfect precision.
Under \emph{Normalised PR Gain} $(\text{PR-AUC} - \pi_s)/(1 - \pi_s)$ --- an
\textbf{exploratory} addition, introduced after the protocol was fixed because
lift has a prevalence-dependent ceiling (Appendix~\ref{app:alignment}), and
which is
algebraically the attainable-lift fraction $\eta$, not an independent
metric --- performance is U-shaped ($0.062 \to 0.026 \to 0.098$), recovering
at Stage 3. The recovery is real but modest in absolute terms: even at its
best the model closes under a tenth of the distance between chance and
perfect precision, and the S3 seed standard deviation ($\pm 0.0086$) is the
largest of the three stages, reflecting that stage's small test partition.
Conversely, prevalence-free \emph{ROC-AUC} drops monotonically from 0.641 to 0.570 with seed standard deviations far smaller than cross-stage differences ($0.0009$--$0.0041$), confirming that the model's pure ranking skill degrades as intent consolidates.
Expected calibration error holds at 0.010--0.017 throughout, so the ranking decline is not a probability calibration artefact.
Two conclusions follow, and it matters that they are kept distinct. The
first is a verdict: H1 predicted improvement and the data show none on the
primary metric, so the hypothesis is rejected, and the ROC-AUC decline
strengthens rather than qualifies that rejection, since it moves in the same
direction on a prevalence-free scale. The second is a caution about
inference: the three normalisations disagree about the shape of the
trajectory, so no single prevalence-adjusted number characterises ``how
predictable'' a stage is. Ranking skill falls monotonically as intent
consolidates; the absolute precision headroom the model captures is smallest
mid-funnel and largest at intent. Both are properties of the same fitted
models, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion-decline} interprets them together
rather than adjudicating between them.

A third caution is more consequential than either, and
Section~\ref{sec:results-cohort} tests it rather than noting it: each stage is
evaluated on a different population, so every number in
Table~\ref{tab:stage-models} confounds what the model knows with who it is
asked about.

\subsection{Separating information from composition (fixed cohort)}
\label{sec:results-cohort}

The stage models are fitted and scored on the sessions that reach each stage.
Those populations differ in size by more than an order of magnitude and in
prevalence by roughly six-fold (Table~\ref{tab:stage-models}), so a
cross-stage comparison mixes two mechanisms. The first is
\emph{information}: a later stage observes a longer prefix and therefore has
more to work with. The second is \emph{composition}: a later stage describes
customers already selected by their own prior behaviour, and that selection
removes much of the between-customer variance a model would otherwise
exploit. Naming the confound does not resolve it, so we test it.

The test holds the evaluation population fixed. Each stage model is trained
exactly as before --- changing training would test a different artefact ---
but all three are then scored on the same sessions: those reaching S3, which
by construction have a defined prefix at every stage, intersected with the
frozen test partition. The result is 821 sessions with one set of labels and
one prevalence, $\pi = 0.463$, shared by all three rows of
Table~\ref{tab:cohort}. This analysis is \textbf{exploratory}: the protocol
specified conditional-population evaluation and did not anticipate a fixed
cohort (Appendix~\ref{app:alignment}).

Inference here must not rest on the seed standard deviations used elsewhere in
this section. Those measure how far the \emph{fit} moves when the random seed
changes; the question is how far the \emph{estimate} would move on another
sample of customers, and on 821 sessions with an AUC near one half the second
is several times the first. Judging a between-stage gap against seed
dispersion is precisely the error the amendment log records under A20b, so the
cohort comparison is made against stratified bootstrap intervals over the
cohort (2{,}000 resamples, shared across stages so that paired differences are
computed on the same resampled customers).

The monotone decline does not survive, and neither does anything that would
replace it. On the conditional populations lift runs
$1.79 \to 1.41 \to 1.09$; on the fixed cohort it runs
$1.047 \to 1.032 \to 1.066$, and \emph{every pairwise interval contains
zero} on both metrics. The three stages are not distinguishable from one
another. They are barely distinguishable from chance: the S1 and S2 intervals
include the no-skill values of 1 and 0.5, and only S3 excludes them, marginally
(lift [1.010, 1.168], ROC-AUC [0.510, 0.590]). The correct summary is that on
a population this homogeneous none of the three models separates buyers from
non-buyers to a useful degree, and the apparent late-stage advantage in the
point estimates is not supported.

That is the finding the section needs. The claim doing work is that the
decline \emph{vanishes}, not that it reverses --- a reversal would require
evidence these data do not contain, and an earlier draft of this section
asserted one by comparing means to seed standard deviations.

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions. The first is
that the headline decline of Section~\ref{sec:results-performance} is
primarily a property of \emph{who is in the sample at each stage}, not of what
the prefix contains. Sessions that reach a cart are, behaviourally, far more
alike than sessions that merely begin, and homogeneity is hostile to ranking:
there is less to rank. The decline is therefore real as a description of what
a deployed stage model faces, because a deployed S3 model does face the S3
population --- but it is not evidence that later prefixes are less
informative, and any theoretical reading that treats it as such is
overreaching. The second consequence is that H1's rejection stands. H1
predicted that prevalence-normalised skill would improve along the funnel in
operation, and it does not; the fixed-cohort result identifies the mechanism
rather than reversing the verdict.

\paragraph{Two bounds on this test.} The first is distributional. The S1 and
S2 models are trained where prevalence is 0.073 and 0.059 and scored where it
is 0.463, which is a prior-probability shift of the kind
Section~\ref{sec:results-sensitivity} shows this setting to be sensitive to.
Their calibration on the cohort is therefore meaningless, and only the
ranking metrics reported above survive, since those are invariant to any
monotone recalibration. Refitting each stage on cohort members alone removes
the shift at the cost of a much smaller training set, and it changes nothing:
lift runs $1.002 \to 1.012 \to 1.047$, every pairwise interval again contains
zero (S3 $-$ S1 lift $+0.053$, [$-0.023$, $+0.138$]; ROC-AUC $+0.024$,
[$-0.027$, $+0.075$]), S1 and S2 again include the no-skill value and S3 again
excludes it marginally. The conclusion is not an artefact of applying a model
outside the prevalence it was trained under.

The second bound is power. 821 sessions is what the intersection of three
stage populations with a held-out month leaves, and the intervals are
correspondingly wide. They are wide enough to establish that the decline is
absent and too wide to establish a small effect in either direction, which is
stated rather than glossed. A third, minor point belongs with them: the
percentile intervals for the \emph{levels} are centred slightly above their
point estimates, because average precision is upward-biased when a small
sample is resampled with replacement. The paired differences, where the bias
enters both terms and largely cancels, are unaffected, and it is the
differences that carry the argument.

\subsection{Feature-family ablation (H3)}
\label{sec:results-ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the pre-specified nine-contrast family

Temporal pacing features contribute measurably at S1 and S2 (+0.0102 and

should not be interpreted, which is why it is described here and not built
upon.

\paragraph{Which of these trajectories carries information?} A Shapley value
is faithful to the model, not to the world, and the stage models have modest
discriminative skill. A model with little skill can be explained perfectly
faithfully while its attributions describe only the covariance structure of
the feature matrix: a block of correlated, high-variance features will absorb
credit whether or not it predicts anything. Faithfulness, stability and
reproducibility are all silent on this, because an attribution can be
faithful, stable, reproducible and empty. We therefore refit each stage on
permuted labels and recompute the same grouped shares against the same
reference grouping, twenty times. Under permutation no feature can carry
information about the outcome, so whatever share survives is what the pipeline
manufactures from feature geometry alone. This test is \textbf{exploratory}.

The null is not near zero, which is the first thing worth reporting: permuted
models still place 41.8\% of attribution on engagement and 39.0\% on price at
S1. Judged against it (Table~\ref{tab:permutation}), the trajectories separate
into three groups.

attribution table and it is \emph{not} distinguishable from the permutation

null at all three stages, and the null reproduces the same rise-then-plateau
shape (41.8, 47.9, 42.9) that the observed trajectory shows (16.9, 36.8, 33.8).

journey.

\textbf{H2 is not supported.} Its behavioural component predicted that
engagement features would gain attribution mass across stages; they do rise,
but the permutation null rises with them and sits above them throughout, so the
observed trajectory is not evidence of the predicted mechanism. Its contextual
component remains untestable, since Dataset~B contains no traffic-source or
device variables and we decline to proxy them. What survives from RQ2 is
narrower and better evidenced than the original hypothesis: attribution does
migrate, and one family---navigation and category behaviour---carries
stage-specific information that peaks mid-funnel and is absent at either end.

The apparent tension with the ablation---navigation entropy receives 22.4\% of
S2 attribution yet adds no detectable unique predictive value---is the
predicted consequence of correlated families sharing information, not a
contradiction: ablation measures unique contribution after accounting for the
rest of the model, while Shapley attribution allocates credit among correlated
features. The permutation null is what distinguishes the two cases, since it
separates credit a family receives because it predicts from credit it receives
because of where it sits in the correlation structure.

Because stage populations differ (Section~\ref{sec:results-descriptive}),
trajectories are read as stage-conditioned explanation profiles rather than
within-session causal evolution; their reliability is established next.

\subsection{What a whole-session analysis reports instead (RQ4)}
\label{sec:results-rq4}

RQ4 asks whether stage conditioning surfaces intervention points that a
static whole-session analysis misses. Answering it requires an actual
whole-session model, so we fitted one. This comparator is
\textbf{exploratory}: the protocol specified a static contrast but not its
form, and an earlier version of this analysis used the arithmetic mean of the
stage shares, which is true by construction and tests nothing
(Appendix~\ref{app:alignment}). The comparator uses the same LightGBM
configuration and the same interventional TreeSHAP, aggregated over the
entire session with no cut-point applied, evaluated on the same frozen
temporal split and aggregated under the same reference grouping.

The comparison must be chosen carefully, because the critique of
whole-session practice bundles two defects that are not equally serious.
The first is \emph{label leakage}: aggregating over a completed session
counts the purchase event itself, so an event count encodes the outcome. The
second is \emph{temporal invalidity}: even with the purchase removed, the
features still describe behaviour occurring after the moment an intervention
would have been taken. Only the second is the defect this paper is about, and
only the second is committed by competent practice. We therefore fit both
models and report the second as the comparator of record
(Table~\ref{tab:whole-session}).

The leakage model confirms that the first defect is trivial and uninteresting.
Given the whole session including its purchase events, LightGBM attains a test
PR-AUC of 0.9995 and a ROC-AUC of 0.9999 at a prevalence of 0.089: it reads
the outcome off the session's own length. Treating that number as the
whole-session benchmark would be a straw man, so it is not used further.

Removing the purchase events leaves the fair comparator. Over the five frozen
seeds it attains a test PR-AUC of $0.6086 \pm 0.0007$ and a ROC-AUC of
$0.8925 \pm 0.0002$ on the same 38,715 test sessions at the same prevalence
--- far above the 0.131 of the strongest stage model, and not attributable to
leakage. The gap measures the value of information the stage
models are forbidden to use: what a customer does \emph{after} the decision
point is highly diagnostic of what they eventually do, which is precisely why
retrospective explanations look so convincing and precisely why they cannot
guide an intervention.

\textbf{RQ4 is answered affirmatively, and the mechanism is more specific than
the question presumed.} A static analysis does not report the stage
trajectory's average, as an earlier and weaker version of this comparison
assumed; it concentrates attribution on how long and how fast a session ran,
because over a whole session those quantities absorb the signal that
stage-conditioned features carry separately. The consequence for practice is
direct: a manager reading the static analysis would conclude that engagement
intensity is the dominant lever at every point in the journey, and would not
learn that price sensitivity dominates at exploration or that category
exploration dominates mid-funnel. One qualification limits how the columns may
be read. The share columns are not a partition of a common vocabulary, since
the whole-session model has features the stage grouping does not cover, so
``flattening'' should be read as the change in a family's prominence rather
than as a conserved transfer of mass.

\subsection{Cross-paradigm agreement (H4)}
\label{sec:results-h4}

The GRU is competitive with the tree models---test PR-AUC 0.124 against
0.131 at S1 and 0.565 against 0.568 at S3---so disagreement below is not
attributable to one incompetent arm.

The pre-specified test was an aggregate one: Spearman correlation between the
two paradigms' concept rankings, against a threshold of 0.6. It cannot be
interpreted, and we report that rather than the number. The schemas share only
four or five mappable concepts, and Spearman on four items takes a handful of
discrete values, so the statistic has almost no resolution and no useful null.
The pre-registration did not anticipate how few concepts the schemas would
share. The aggregate values are given in Table~\ref{tab:h4} for completeness
and carry no weight here.

\textbf{H4 is therefore assessed on the per-instance analysis}, which is
well-powered: for each shared concept, tree and sequence attributions are
correlated across 350+ shared sessions, over five seeds.

% [tab:h4 moved to supplementary material]

Per-instance agreement is indistinguishable from zero at every stage, and
only 3 of 14 (stage, concept) pairs hold a consistent sign across seeds.
\textbf{The two paradigms do not agree about individual customers}, and this
is the finding that bears on practice: a system surfacing per-customer
explanations is making a claim the evidence does not support, even where the
same models rank concepts similarly in aggregate. The comparison
conflates differences in architecture, input representation and explanation
algorithm, and the observed disagreement is accordingly an upper bound on
conceptual divergence rather than a pure measure of it; quantifying the
within-method variability of repeated TimeSHAP runs would sharpen the
reference point and is left to future work. We note explicitly that a
single-seed version of this analysis produced an apparent $-0.151$ at S2
that did \emph{not} survive reseeding (Appendix~A); with seed standard
deviations of 0.2--0.4 on these correlations, sign stability rather than
the mean is the reportable quantity.

\subsection{Robustness and sensitivity analyses}
\label{sec:results-sensitivity}

\paragraph{Sessionisation gap} Halving or doubling the 30-minute threshold
changes the session count by $\pm 3\%$, stage reach by under one percentage
point, and stage prevalence by well under half a point
(Table~\ref{tab:gap})---all far smaller than the between-stage differences
on which the paper's claims rest.

% [tab:gap moved to supplementary material]

\paragraph{Sample size} Doubling the subsample to 400{,}000 users changes
PR-AUC by $+0.003$ to $+0.009$ and leaves every conclusion intact: lift
still falls monotonically (1.84, 1.49, 1.10) and ROC-AUC with it (0.648,
0.634, 0.570). The sample is nested at a fixed seed, so the differences are
attributable to the added users rather than to a different draw.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter tuning} Refitting the stage models with the
Optuna-selected configurations (Section~\ref{sec:methods-tuning}) moves
PR-AUC by $+0.002$ (S1) and $+0.003$ (S2)---within one to two seed standard
deviations---and by $+0.032$ at S3 ($0.568 \to 0.600$, roughly eight seed
standard deviations). The lift ordering is unchanged: 1.81, 1.46, 1.15
tuned against 1.79, 1.41, 1.09 at defaults, still monotonically falling.
All three searches selected the minimum tree count in the space with strong
regularisation---shallow trees at S1 and S3, a slow learning rate at
S2---consistent with a drift-prone temporal split rewarding conservative
models; that the largest gain lands at the underpowered S3 fits the same
reading, and even tuned it remains the weakest stage on lift.

\paragraph{Split protocol} On the grouped split, which is identity-clean
but not time-ordered, lift falls $1.98 \to 1.53 \to 1.15$. The monotone
decline on which H1's rejection rests is therefore not an artefact of the
temporal protocol. Grouped performance sits above temporal at every stage,
which is what temporal drift predicts: a model tested on its own period's
peers faces an easier problem than one tested on the future.

\paragraph{Calibration set} Fitting the calibrator on the validation month
rather than on a slice of the training period raises expected calibration
error from 0.025 to 0.118. Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} shows why the
failure is systematic rather than noisy: the validation-calibrated
reliability curve falls below the identity line in \emph{every} probability
bin---observed conversion rates are uniformly lower than predicted
probabilities---which is the signature of a calibrator transported from a
higher-prevalence population (November, 25.4\%) to a lower-prevalence one
(December, 12.5\%), while the training-slice calibrator (12.2\%) tracks the
identity line closely throughout. The figure is thus a compact empirical demonstration of
prior-probability shift, and the reasons the textbook choice of calibration
set inverts under drift are developed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-method}.

\section{Decision-support interpretation}
\label{sec:decision-support}
%==============================================================================

The results so far are predictive and explanatory. This section converts them
into decision-support terms, because a conversion model earns its place in an
operational system not by ranking well but by changing what an organisation
does. Figure~\ref{fig:decision-support} sets out the architecture the
framework implies.

\emph{Status of the evidence in this section.} The error analysis against a
trivial targeting rule (Section~\ref{sec:why-not-prediction}) and the decision
economics derived from it (Section~\ref{sec:decision-economics}) are
\textbf{exploratory}: neither was pre-specified in the protocol. They are
presented prominently because we judge them the study's most consequential
decision-support findings, and labelled exploratory because they are. A reader
who weights confirmatory and exploratory evidence differently should weight
this section accordingly; Appendix~\ref{app:alignment} lists every such
addition in one place.

\centering

\subsection{Why prediction alone is not decision support here}
\label{sec:why-not-prediction}

A model earns its place in an operational system by beating the one comparator
available to every organisation for free: the trivial rule that flags every
session, whose $F_1$ is $2\pi_s/(1+\pi_s)$ at prevalence $\pi_s$. Judged that
way, the stages diverge sharply --- and the divergence turns out to be
governed by the threshold rule rather than by the models, which is the point
of this subsection. Table~\ref{tab:error} reports the confusion structure at
each stage's validation-selected operating point.

% [tab:error moved to supplementary material]

At its own operating point the intent-stage model refers 99.0\% of sessions
and beats the blanket rule by 0.0008 $F_1$; the earlier stages refer 17.4\%
and 24.3\% and beat it by 0.0647 and 0.0359, at roughly six and ten false
positives per true positive. Read at face value this says late-stage targeting
is worthless, and that reading is an artefact of how the threshold was chosen.
$F_1$ maximisation is prevalence-sensitive: as $\pi_s$ approaches one half the
criterion is increasingly satisfied by flagging everything, and
$\pi_{S3} = 0.521$. A rule that flags 99\% of sessions cannot beat a rule that
flags 100\% by much, whatever model sits behind it.

The durable observation is narrower and more useful: decision value and
ranking quality are not the same quantity, and neither is a property of the
model alone. Any of PR-AUC, $F_1$ or accuracy read in isolation would have
ranked S3 the strongest stage; measured against a decision-relevant null at a
threshold a standard rule selected, it looks worthless. Both readings are
incomplete, because the operating point is a free variable that neither
reports. Section~\ref{sec:decision-economics} makes it explicit and sweeps it.

Three implications follow that a whole-session analysis could not have
produced. First, the navigation peak at consideration (3.5\%, 22.4\%, 7.4\%

whole-session comparator of Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4} assigns the family
4.1\% and reports no window at all. Second, the explanation-quality profile does \emph{not} differ
detectably across stages (Section~\ref{sec:results-quality}): the three
faithfulness values lie within a fifth of a standard deviation of one another,
so a system should attach the same --- moderate --- confidence at every stage
rather than a stage-varying one. Third, the near-zero
per-instance agreement between the tree and recurrent arms
(Section~\ref{sec:results-h4}) bears directly on interface design: a system
that surfaces per-customer explanations to an analyst is making a claim that
this evidence does not support, even though the same models agree at the
population level.

\subsection{Decision economics: what the operating points imply for budget}
\label{sec:decision-economics}

Section~\ref{sec:why-not-prediction} establishes that the stages differ in
selectivity. This subsection turns that into the quantity a manager allocating
a contact budget actually needs. No cost or margin figures exist for this
retailer, and none are invented here; everything below is expressed per unit
of contact cost $c$ and per-conversion margin $m$, which the reader supplies.
What the data \emph{do} fix is precision and prevalence, and those alone
determine both the break-even condition and the relative standing of the
stages.

Contacting a flagged session is worthwhile when the expected gain exceeds the
cost, $\text{precision} \times u \times m > c$, where $u$ is the probability
that the intervention converts a session that would otherwise not have
converted. This yields a break-even uplift of $u^{*} = c / (\text{precision}
\cdot m)$. But that comparison is against \emph{not contacting}, which is the
wrong null: the alternative available to every organisation is to contact
everyone, which requires no model. Against that null the relevant quantity is
the \emph{incremental} precision, $\text{precision} - \pi_s$, and the
break-even becomes $u^{*}_{\text{inc}} = c / \big( (\text{precision} - \pi_s)
\cdot m \big)$. Table~\ref{tab:economics} reports both.

% [tab:economics moved to supplementary material]

The final two columns carry the argument at these operating points. To obtain
one conversion beyond what a blanket policy already delivers, an organisation
must contact roughly 15 sessions at exploration and 36 at consideration. Both
figures are stable across seeds, and both incremental precisions sit eight or
more standard deviations from zero.

S3 is different in kind at its own operating point. Its incremental precision
is $+0.0039 \pm 0.0035$, barely one standard deviation from zero, and the
per-seed contact requirement ranges from 104 to over 34{,}000. The honest
statement is therefore not that cart-stage targeting costs 257 contacts per
incremental conversion but that \emph{at the $F_1$-selected threshold its
incremental value is not distinguishable from zero, so the cost per
incremental conversion is unbounded above}. An earlier version of this
analysis reported a single-seed point estimate of 417, which conveyed a
precision the five-seed evidence does not support; the instability is the
finding, and it is reported rather than averaged away.

\paragraph{The operating point is doing much of the work.} Every figure above
is conditional on the threshold rule, and at S3 that rule flags 99\% of
sessions. That is a near-blanket policy, and incremental value over a blanket
rule necessarily approaches zero as the flag rate approaches one, whatever
the model. Whether late-stage targeting is worthwhile is
therefore a question the $F_1$ threshold cannot answer, and we answer it by
sweeping the flag rate directly. Table~\ref{tab:sweep} reports incremental
precision at each of a range of budgets, holding the models fixed. This sweep
is \textbf{exploratory}; the protocol did not specify it.

The sweep changes the practical conclusion. At a 10\% budget the intent stage
delivers the largest incremental precision of the three
($0.1137 \pm 0.0130$, against $0.0930 \pm 0.0016$ at exploration and
$0.0376 \pm 0.0077$ at consideration), and it remains the highest at every
budget from 5\% upward. Converted to contacts, one conversion beyond the
blanket rule costs 8.8 contacts at intent, 10.8 at exploration and 26.6 at
consideration. The claim that late-stage targeting adds nothing is an artefact
of the $F_1$ threshold and is withdrawn.

Two things do survive, and they are not the same claim. The first is that
ranking skill declines: top-decile lift falls monotonically from
$2.27\times$ at exploration to $1.64\times$ and $1.22\times$, so the intent
model orders sessions least well of the three even where it targets best. The
two facts are compatible because lift is measured against a prevalence that
differs six-fold, while incremental precision is measured in conversions per
contact, which is what a budget buys. The second is that exploration dominates
at small budgets: at a 1\% flag rate its incremental precision is
$0.2148 \pm 0.0022$, roughly seven times the intent stage's, and its estimate
is far more stable --- the intent stage's standard deviation at that budget
($\pm 0.0767$) exceeds its mean, because a 1\% flag over a 2,973-session test
partition selects only about thirty sessions.

The managerial statement the evidence supports is therefore conditional on
budget rather than universal. Under a scarce contact budget, target early:
exploration is both the most selective stage and the one where intervention
is cheapest and most reversible. Under a budget large enough to reach a tenth
or more of eligible sessions, the intent stage is the most efficient place to
spend the marginal contact, notwithstanding that its ranking is the weakest.
The consideration stage is the least attractive at every budget tested, which
is consistent with its anomalous behaviour throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:s2-problem}).

\paragraph{Does tuning change the picture?} The headline models use default
hyperparameters, and a natural objection is that a tuned model would behave
differently, particularly at S3 where tuning raises PR-AUC most (0.568 to
0.600). At the $F_1$-selected operating point it does not help: refitting with
the Optuna-selected configurations moves S3's incremental precision from
$+0.0039 \pm 0.0035$ to $+0.0017 \pm 0.0017$, so the mean contact requirement
rises from 257 to roughly 600 with an unbounded upper range, even though
ROC-AUC improves from 0.570 to 0.601. At S2, tuning helps on both counts
(contacts fall from 35.5 to 25.6).

The divergence is instructive but narrower than it first appears. Because it
is measured at a threshold that flags 99\% of S3 sessions, it shows that
tuning for a ranking criterion does not fix a badly chosen operating point ---
not that tuning is harmful. The substantive point stands in either reading:
a practitioner who tuned for PR-AUC and stopped there would have concluded
that the intent stage had improved as a targeting system, and would have had
no evidence for it. What the flag-rate sweep adds is that the operating point,
not the hyperparameters, is what such a practitioner should have examined
first.

Two further boundaries on this calculation should be stated rather than left implicit.
First, it assumes the intervention's uplift $u$ does not itself vary by stage.
That is unlikely to hold --- a customer at the cart may be more responsive to
a well-timed nudge than a browser at entry --- and a stage-varying $u$ would
shift the comparison in Table~\ref{tab:sweep} by a factor the data cannot
supply. The calculation identifies how large that compensating effect would
have to be, which is a testable quantity, rather than assuming it away.
Second, the analysis treats all
conversions as equal in value; if cart-stage sessions carry systematically
higher basket values, margin $m$ is itself stage-dependent and the comparison
would need to be re-weighted. Neither correction is available in these data,
and both are recorded as such.

\subsection{What a deploying organisation should take from this}
\label{sec:deployment}

The framework's value is not that it improves conversion prediction --- it
does not, and the tuning analysis shows the headline numbers are close to
what default hyperparameters already achieve. Its value is that it makes
visible four things a conventional pipeline hides: that predictive skill and
prevalence move together and must be separated before a stage is judged; that
much of the apparent cross-stage difference is composition rather than
information, so a fixed-cohort check belongs in any funnel evaluation; that
attribution mass migrates, so the same model gives different advice at
different points in the journey; and that explanation quality is itself a
measurable property that varies by stage.

The operational conclusion is a rule for choosing operating points rather
than a ranking of stages. Selecting a threshold by $F_1$ produced a
cart-stage policy that flagged 99\% of sessions and added nothing over
flagging everyone; selecting it by budget produced the most efficient
targeting of the three stages at that same model. An organisation adopting
this framework should therefore expect to conclude, as we do here, that the
question ``which stage should we target?'' is under-specified until the
contact budget is named. That is a decision-support result, and a
conventional evaluation would not have surfaced it.

\subsection{Why ranking skill declines as intent forms}
\label{sec:discussion-decline}

The clearest result contradicts the pre-specified hypothesis: predictive
performance does not improve along the funnel, and on the prevalence-free
scale it deteriorates. Because this is a negative result against a
directional expectation, it deserves a substantive rather than merely
statistical interpretation. Two quantities must be held apart first. Ranking
skill---who is more likely to convert than whom---falls monotonically.
Precision headroom captured over chance is U-shaped, recovering at intent.
These are not in conflict: at a base rate of 52\% a model can place many
converters above many non-converters while ordering them poorly, because
the bar chance already clears is high. The interpretation below concerns
ranking skill, which is the quantity an intervention system actually
consumes when it decides whom to target.

The pattern admits a selection reading: each successive stage conditions on
an increasingly committed population, and conditioning on commitment removes
precisely the behavioural variance that discriminates purchasers from
non-purchasers. Early in the journey, broad exploratory signals separate
populations that differ widely in intent; by the time a cart exists, nearly
everyone remaining looks intent, and the residual outcome uncertainty is
governed by factors the clickstream does not observe---price comparison
off-site, household deliberation, payment friction.

Unlike most such readings, this one is testable within the present data, and
Section~\ref{sec:results-cohort} tests it. If the decline were a property of
what the prefix contains, it would persist when every model is scored on the
same customers; if it were a property of who remains, it would disappear. It
disappears. On a single cohort of sessions reaching every stage, lift is flat
to within roughly three seed standard deviations
($1.047 \to 1.032 \to 1.066$) and ROC-AUC rises slightly rather than falling.
The selection account is therefore supported and the alternative---that later
prefixes carry less usable information---is not. Two implications follow. The
theoretical one is that the funnel effect documented here belongs to the
sampling structure of staged journeys rather than to the information content
of behavioural sequences, which narrows what may be generalised from it: any
setting in which stages are defined by customer self-selection should show
the same pattern, and a setting in which stages are assigned exogenously
should not. The operational one is that the decline is nonetheless what a
deployed system experiences, because a deployed intent-stage model is asked
about the intent-stage population and no other. The framework's remaining
falsifiable prediction is unchanged: covariates capturing off-stream context
should recover discrimination at S3 where within-stream behaviour cannot.
Testing it requires data this study does not possess.

The finding qualifies a common operational assumption without reversing it.
The stages where prediction is conventionally deployed (late, high-intent)
are the stages where a model adds least ranking skill over the base rate, and
prevalence-normalised reporting is what makes this visible; absolute PR-AUC
alone would have told the opposite, wrong, story. But weak ranking is not the
same as weak decision value, and Section~\ref{sec:decision-economics} shows
that at budgets above roughly a twentieth of eligible sessions the intent
stage yields the most conversions per contact of the three despite ranking
worst. The conclusion an organisation should draw is about \emph{how to
choose an operating point}, not about which stage to abandon.
Figure~\ref{fig:contributions} traces this logic for all four research
questions, mapping each from the methodological component that makes it
answerable, through the empirical finding, to the theoretical claim it
licenses. The map makes the design-science character of the study explicit:
each finding is produced by precisely the component---prefix-only features
with lift as the cross-stage metric, stage-conditioned attribution,
pre-specified validation with per-instance agreement, the static
contrast---that conventional pipelines omit, which is the sense in which
the artifact, and not merely the dataset, generates the knowledge. The
map's bottom line is also its epistemic posture: three of the four
pre-specified hypotheses were not supported, and all four are reported as
found.

\subsection{Attribution migration and the staged journey}
\label{sec:discussion-migration}

The migration results give quantitative content to a long-standing
theoretical claim: journey stages are qualitatively distinct decision
regimes \citep{bucklin2003model, lemon2016understanding}. The mid-funnel
peak of navigation entropy is the sharpest illustration---comparison
behaviour is maximally informative exactly when the staged account says
customers compare---and it is structurally invisible to whole-session
attribution, which assigns the family 4.1\% while placing 45.5\% of its own
explanatory weight on how long and how fast the session ran
(Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4}).
The coexistence of high attribution share with negligible unique predictive
contribution (Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}) is itself informative:
attribution answers ``what evidence does the model lean on,'' ablation
answers ``what could not be replaced,'' and conflating the two reads
redundancy as importance or importance as redundancy. Reporting both
quantities side by side, as the ablation ladder and the migration table do
here, makes the distinction inspectable rather than leaving it implicit.

Beyond confirming that stages differ, the evidence suggests a sharper
theoretical claim about \emph{what kind} of object a journey stage is. The
staged-journey literature treats stages primarily as phases of an unfolding
intention: the customer moves from awareness to consideration to intent, and
the behavioural signature of each phase reflects a different mental state
\citep{bucklin2003model, moe2003buying, lemon2016understanding}. Our results
are consistent with that account but add a constraint it does not supply.
Predictive skill and attribution structure move in opposite directions:
normalised skill falls monotonically (lift 1.79 to 1.09; ROC-AUC 0.641 to
0.570) while attribution becomes \emph{more} concentrated and more distinctive,
with engagement doubling its share and navigation peaking mid-funnel. A stage
is therefore not simply a point at which more is known about the customer.
It is a point at which the population has been progressively filtered by the
customer's own prior actions, and filtering removes precisely the variance
that discriminates outcomes while leaving --- indeed sharpening --- the
behavioural signature that describes what remains.

This distinction between \emph{describing} a stage and \emph{discriminating}
within it is, we suggest, the theoretically useful reading of the migration
result, and it has a corollary the staged-journey literature does not draw:
the informativeness of a behavioural signal and its usefulness for
intervention are not merely different, they are inversely related along the
funnel in these data. The strongest description arrives where the weakest
targeting value lies. If that pattern generalises, it implies that
journey-stage theory and targeting practice have been reading the same
construct for different purposes without noticing that the purposes pull
apart --- and that a framework which reports only one of the two quantities
will systematically mislocate where intervention is worthwhile. That is a
falsifiable claim, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion-decline} states the
off-stream covariate test that would put it at risk.

\subsection{The consideration problem}
\label{sec:s2-problem}

One stage resists the framework's own account of itself, and the difficulty
is worth stating directly rather than distributing across the results.
Consideration (S2) is where the attribution structure is most distinctive:
navigation entropy rises from 3.5\% to 22.4\% of attribution mass and falls
again to 7.4\%, the single non-monotone trajectory in the study and the one
that most clearly identifies an intervention window. It is also the stage at
which the explanations are least trustworthy, though on one measure rather
than the two an earlier reading claimed. Per-instance cross-paradigm
agreement is $-0.018$, the only negative value, with no concept holding a
consistent sign across seeds. Faithfulness is 0.494, nominally below the
pre-registered threshold of 0.5, but the shortfall is 0.03 of a standard
deviation and the gap to the best stage four tenths of one
(Section~\ref{sec:results-quality}); it is recorded as a pre-specified
failure and not treated as evidence. What does distinguish S2 substantively
is its predictive standing: the weakest lift of the three (1.41), the lowest
incremental precision at every contact budget tested
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep}), and roughly 31 contacts per incremental conversion
at its own operating point.

The stage where the explanation is most informative is therefore the stage
where it is least reliable. Three readings are compatible with the evidence
and the data cannot separate them. The first is statistical: S2's population
is defined by a milestone that intent-rich sessions bypass
(Section~\ref{sec:results-descriptive}), so it is a residual category, and
residual categories are heterogeneous in ways that depress both predictive
signal and explanation fidelity. The second is representational: comparison
behaviour may be genuinely multi-modal --- some customers narrow, others
broaden --- so that a single attribution vector averages over qualitatively
different journeys and is faithful to none of them. The third is
methodological: navigation entropy is zero for a prefix confined to a single
category and only becomes informative once several categories have been
visited, so its S2 peak might be a property of when the feature becomes
computable rather than of when the behaviour matters.

The third reading is testable, and because it would attenuate the study's most
striking attribution finding we tested it rather than leaving it as a caveat.
Table~\ref{tab:entropy-availability} reports, per stage, the share of explained
prefixes for which entropy is non-degenerate, together with the attribution
share computed overall and computed only on those prefixes. Availability does
rise from S1 to S2, from 16.5\% to 38.7\% of prefixes, so part of the concern
is well founded. But conditioning on availability does not remove the peak: it
sharpens it. Among prefixes where entropy is actually defined, the family
carries 1.8\% of attribution at S1, 20.2\% at S2 and 4.3\% at S3 --- an
eleven-fold gap between S1 and S2 that cannot be explained by the feature
being computable more often, because both figures are computed only where it
is computable. This analysis is \textbf{exploratory}; it was prompted by the
alternative explanation rather than pre-specified.

% [tab:entropy-availability moved to supplementary material]

The behavioural reading therefore survives the correction, though with a
qualification worth stating: availability and attribution peak at the same
stage, and no design that measures entropy on observed prefixes can fully
separate ``customers compare here'' from ``comparison becomes measurable
here''. What the test establishes is the weaker but sufficient claim that the
peak is not \emph{merely} definitional.

For deployment the consequence is uncomfortable but clear. The stage that
most invites a targeted intervention is the stage at which the reason offered
for that intervention should carry least weight. A system that surfaced
stage-conditioned explanations to an analyst would need to attach different
confidence to S2 than to S1 or S3 --- and, on the per-instance evidence,
would need to withhold customer-level explanations at S2 altogether. This is
the concrete form of the paper's broader argument that explanation quality is
an empirical property to be measured per stratum rather than assumed for a
model as a whole.

\subsection{Implications for decision support and practice}
\label{sec:discussion-practice}

Four implications follow for intelligent decision support. First,
prediction and explanation should share informational constraints by
architecture, not convention: an explanation layer bolted onto a
whole-session model can cite behaviour that postdates the decision point,
and no amount of visualisation repairs that. Second, intervention design
can key on the stage-conditioned profiles, but only on the parts of them that
clear a permutation null: browsing breadth at exploration, favouring
assortment interventions, and cross-category comparison at consideration,
favouring comparison support. The families with the largest nominal shares ---
price context and engagement --- do not clear it, and an intervention designed
around them would be designed around the correlation structure of the feature
matrix. That distinction is not visible in a conventional attribution report,
and drawing it is part of what the validation layer is for. Third, an operating point is a decision variable and
should be reported as one. The same intent-stage model looks worthless or
best-in-class depending on whether its threshold is chosen by $F_1$ or by
budget, so a decision-support system should expose the flag rate to the
manager rather than fix it inside a model-selection step. Fourth,
explanation
validation belongs in deployment pipelines: the per-stage quality table is
cheap to compute, and in these data it would have flagged S2 as the stage
where explanations deserve least trust---something no aggregate metric
reveals. The per-instance H4 result adds a governance caution: aggregate
agreement between explanation methods does not license per-customer
narratives, and systems that surface individual explanations should
validate at the instance level, not the population level.

\subsection{Methodological implications}
\label{sec:discussion-method}

Two findings generalise beyond this study. The calibration result is a
concrete instance of prior-probability shift \citep{saerens2002adjusting}:
under temporal drift, the validation month is the \emph{worst} place to fit
a calibrator, because its prevalence differs from both training and test;
an ECE of 0.118 versus 0.025 is the measured cost of the textbook choice in
these data. The tuning result carries a related message: under a temporal
split, hyperparameter search consistently selected the most regularised
configurations available, and the gain concentrated in the smallest, most
drift-exposed stage. Both are symptoms of the same underlying regime---
evaluation under distribution shift---and both argue that protocol details
conventionally treated as boilerplate (where the calibrator is fitted,
whether defaults are tuned) interact with drift in ways that are large
relative to the effects under study.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations of this study fall into three groups: what the data cannot
express, what the design conditions on, and what the evaluation cannot yet
establish.

The first group concerns measurement. Search-query text is unavailable in
both datasets, scroll depth is proxied by dwell time, and device and
traffic-source variables are absent from Dataset~B, which is why H2's
contextual arm is reported as untestable rather than approximated through
proxies. Dataset~A, for its part, is non-causal by construction: its
features are whole-session aggregates and \texttt{PageValues} is measured
post-transaction, so it functions in this study strictly as a benchmark and
static comparator, never as an instantiation of the framework.

The second group concerns conditioning and power. The stage models are
conditional on reaching the stage, so the improvement curve is a
conditional claim, and Table~\ref{tab:stages} carries the reach rates and
prevalences without which it cannot be read correctly. The intent stage is
underpowered---with 2{,}973 test sessions no S3 ablation contrast is
decidable---and the temporal split, by keeping visitors whole while
respecting time order, costs 51{,}256 of 200{,}000 users as
boundary-straddlers, retaining 222{,}268 of 485{,}459 sessions. Both
limitations share a single remedy, an observation window longer than one
month, which we regard as the highest-value extension of the present
design. The recurrent arm is likewise bounded: it is trained on a subsample of
up to 60{,}000 sessions per stage rather than the full cohort, its
attributions carry seed standard deviations of 0.2--0.4, and one single-seed
conclusion drawn from it had to be withdrawn
(Appendix~A)---an episode that motivates the sign-stability reporting
standard adopted in Section~\ref{sec:results-h4}.

The third group concerns the boundaries of the evaluation itself. The
validation partition is used twice: hyperparameter tuning selects among
models on validation PR-AUC, and the operating threshold is subsequently
chosen on the same partition. The interaction is weak, since tuning
optimises a threshold-free metric and thresholding is a downstream choice
on the already-selected model, but it is a double use, and a stricter
design would reserve a separate partition for each. More fundamentally, the
framework is predictive, not causal: SHAP quantifies contribution to model
predictions, not causal influence on purchasing, so the trajectories
describe how predictive evidence evolves, not how motives do. The same
boundary applies to the decision analysis. Incremental precision measures how
much better than a blanket rule a model selects, which is necessary for a
targeting decision and not sufficient for one: without randomised exposure
the data cannot say whether a selected session would have converted anyway,
so no treatment effect is identified and the results are not uplift estimates
(Section~\ref{sec:related-uplift}). Two further parameters are unavailable
here and would change the ordering if they varied enough by stage: the
responsiveness of customers to the intervention, and the basket value of the
conversions each stage delivers. Finally,
external validity is bounded by the empirical setting---one retailer, one
market, one month, offline evaluation. The framework is portable; these
specific attribution trajectories are not claimed to be, and live
validation of stage-conditioned interventions remains future work.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

Beyond the extensions above: multi-month windows to relieve S3's power
limitation and reduce boundary attrition; off-stream covariates to test the
falsifiable prediction of Section~\ref{sec:discussion-decline}; finer stage
lattices approaching per-event explanation trajectories; within-method
variability baselines for cross-paradigm comparison; and deployment studies
measuring whether stage-conditioned explanations change intervention
decisions. The most consequential extension is a randomised one. The
flag-rate sweep establishes which stage selects best under a given budget but
cannot establish which stage \emph{responds} best, and a field experiment
randomising exposure within stage would supply the missing quantity, allowing
stage-conditioned attribution to be joined to uplift estimation rather than
to propensity alone.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

This study asked what happens when explanation is held to the same temporal
standard as prediction. The answer, on 42.4 million events, is that most of
what a whole-session attribution reports is either unavailable at decision
time or an average over regimes the journey keeps distinct. Funnel-Stage
SHAP enforces the prefix constraint at the representation level, conditions
both model and explanation on the funnel stage, and validates the resulting
explanations for faithfulness, stability and reproducibility. Applied
end-to-end, it made visible evidence that refuted one pre-specified
expectation (predictive performance does not improve along the funnel;
ranking skill falls, and the direction of the effect depends on which
normalisation is applied),
quantified a second (attribution mass migrates, with navigation behaviour
peaking mid-funnel), and split a third (explanation paradigms agree in
aggregate and disagree per instance). Each finding is invisible to the
standard pipeline: the first without reporting chance level alongside
performance, the second without stage conditioning, the third without
instance-level validation.

Two further analyses, prompted by the results rather than pre-specified,
determine how the first finding should be read. Scoring every stage model on
one fixed cohort shows that the decline is a property of who reaches each
stage, not of what a longer prefix contains: a funnel evaluation that omits
this check will attribute to information what belongs to selection. Sweeping
the contact budget shows that the decision value of a stage and its ranking
quality are separate quantities that can point in opposite directions --- the
intent stage ranks worst of the three and, at budgets of a twentieth of
sessions or more, targets best. Both results narrow the claim the study can
make and sharpen the recommendation it can offer.

The broader argument is that explanation quality tracks predictive signal
and should be validated where decisions are made---per stage, and where
individual customers are concerned, per instance. As machine learning
systems increasingly operate on evolving behavioural streams, temporal
fidelity is not an optional refinement of explainable AI but a precondition
for explanations that support, rather than merely decorate, operational
decisions.

\section*{Data and code availability}
Dataset~A is publicly available from the UCI Machine Learning Repository.
Dataset~B (REES46 multi-category store, October 2019) is publicly
distributed; provenance hashes, the frozen splits, the seed list, all
result tables and the full analysis code accompany this manuscript in an
anonymised repository whose persistent identifier will be supplied on
acceptance.

point reading splits frozen to disk before any model was fitted, and runs
are logged with their parameters, metrics and output tables. A pipeline
specification documents the dependency structure of the analysis; it is an
accurate description of the commands that generated the results rather than
a self-contained rebuild, since the raw 42.4-million-event log is not
redistributed with the repository.
